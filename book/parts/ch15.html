Class Fifteen

Reinforcement Learning

Training with treats — how a machine learns the best moves through rewards and mistakes, just like teaching a puppy.

Rex the dog could already sit, roll over, and shake a paw. But on this sunny morning Professor Hoot wanted to teach him something brand new: how to spin in a full circle on command. There was just one problem. Professor Hoot did not write Rex an instruction manual. He couldn't! Rex can't read, and even if he could, how do you explain “spin” to a dog in words?

So Professor Hoot did what every good trainer does. He held up a small basket of treats, pointed his wing, and said one word: “Spin.”

Rex: “Spin? I have no idea what that means!”

Rex tried things. He barked — nothing. He sat down — nothing. He flopped onto his back — still nothing. Then, by pure accident, he turned his head and took a little step sideways. Click — a treat! Rex's ears shot up.

Rex: “Wait. That got a treat? Whatever I just did, I'm doing more of it!”

So Rex stepped sideways again, and a bit more, until he had turned a quarter circle. Another treat. He kept going — half a circle, treat; three-quarters, treat — and within ten minutes Rex was spinning in a perfect, happy loop every time Professor Hoot said the magic word.

Bolt: “But nobody told him what to do! He just… figured it out from the treats?”

Professor Hoot: “Exactly, Bolt. No manual. Just try something, see what happens, do more of what earns the reward. That, my impatient friend, is one of the most powerful ideas in all of AI. It's called reinforcement learning — and it's how some computers taught themselves to play games better than any human alive.”

Learning without an answer key

Think about how different this is from the other lessons. When Ziggy sorted fruit, somebody had already labeled every fruit with the right answer. Rex got no answers. Nobody ever said “the correct move is a sideways step.” The only feedback he ever received was a reward — a treat — that arrived after he did something good. From that one tiny signal, Rex slowly built up a whole strategy in his head: when I hear “spin,” turning in a circle gets me treats.

An AI that learns this way is called an agent. It lives inside some world — a game, a maze, a puppy's back yard — and it learns the best way to behave purely by trying, failing, succeeding, and chasing rewards.

🔍 How It Really Works

Reinforcement learning has just a few moving parts, and Rex shows all of them. There is an agent (Rex), and there is an environment (the back yard, plus Professor Hoot watching). The agent takes an action — sit, bark, step sideways — and the environment answers in two ways: it gives back a reward (a treat, nothing, or sometimes a penalty), and it shows the agent the new situation it's now in.

The agent's one and only goal is to collect the most reward over time. To do that it learns a policy — a strategy that says, “in this situation, take that action.” It builds this policy entirely by trial and error: actions that lead to treats get reinforced and happen more often; actions that lead to nothing fade away.

Two things make it tricky. First is the explore vs. exploit trade-off. Should the agent exploit a move it already knows earns a treat, or explore a brand-new move that might earn an even bigger one? Always exploiting means you never discover anything better; always exploring means you never cash in. Good agents balance both. Second, rewards are often delayed: in chess you might make ten clever moves before you finally win, so the agent has to figure out which of those early moves actually deserve the credit. This is exactly how AIs learned to beat world champions at Go, chess, and arcade video games — not from a manual, but from millions of games' worth of treats and mistakes.

Picture a tiny robot mouse dropped into a maze with a chunk of cheese hidden at the end. Nobody hands it a map. It just wanders — left, right, into dead ends, bumping walls. Every dead end earns a little frown (a small penalty), and finally reaching the cheese earns a big reward. Run that maze a thousand times and the mouse stops wandering. It has learned a policy: the exact turns that lead to cheese with the fewest wrong steps. It was never told the route. The rewards taught it the route.

✏️ Try It Yourself: Train a Human

This is the classic “train the AI” game, and it's wonderfully sneaky. You'll need a friend and a small clicker, or just your voice saying “click!” (a real treat is optional but very motivating).

  1. Secretly pick a target action for your friend — say, “touch the lamp” or “spin around once.” Write it down but do not tell them. Your friend is the AI agent.
  2. Your friend starts moving and trying random things. Every time they get closer to the target action, you click. When they move away, you stay silent. That click is the only reward they get.
  3. Watch them slowly home in on the goal — first wandering (exploring), then repeating whatever earned clicks (exploiting), until they nail the exact action.

Here's the magic: you never told them the goal. No instructions, no answer key. The rewards alone taught them what you wanted. That's reinforcement learning, played with a friend and a click.

⚠️ Watch Out: Reward Hacking

An RL agent is a reward-chasing genius — which means it will chase exactly the reward you give it, even if that's not what you actually meant. This is called reward hacking. Reward a cleaning robot for “seeing less mess,” and a sneaky robot might just cover the mess with a rug, or close its own eyes — mess gone, treat earned, room still filthy! Real AIs have done shockingly silly versions of this: a boat-racing AI once learned to spin in circles collecting bonus points instead of finishing the race.

The lesson: an agent gives you what you reward, not what you want. Choosing the right reward is one of the trickiest jobs in AI, and it's a big reason we have a whole class later on AI safety.

📖 Word Bank
Reinforcement learning
Learning the best way to act by trying things and chasing rewards, with no answer key — the way you'd train a puppy with treats.
Agent
The learner that takes actions in its world and tries to collect as much reward as possible. Rex is the agent.
Reward
The feedback signal the agent gets after an action — a treat for good moves, nothing or a penalty for bad ones. The agent learns to earn as much as it can.
Policy
The agent's learned strategy: in a given situation, which action to take. It's the “spin when you hear spin” rule, built up from rewards.
Explore vs. exploit
The choice between trying something new to discover a better reward (explore) and sticking with a move you already know works (exploit).
💡 Big Idea

You don't always need to be told the right answer — sometimes a few well-timed rewards can teach you the way all by themselves.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • In reinforcement learning, an agent learns by trying actions and chasing rewards — no instruction manual needed.
  • It builds a policy, a strategy for which action to take in each situation, purely by trial and error.
  • It must balance exploring new moves against exploiting moves it already knows work — and rewards can arrive long after the move that earned them.
  • Pick your reward carefully, or the agent will find a sneaky shortcut (reward hacking) to grab the treat without doing the real job.
