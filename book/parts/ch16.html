Search & Pathfinding

Finding the Way Home — how a map app, or a character in a video game, finds the shortest path through a maze without trying every single one.

Pip the mouse was, to put it kindly, completely lost. Professor Hoot had built an enormous hedge maze behind the Academy — tall green walls, twisting corridors, dead ends everywhere — and somewhere on the far side sat the most magnificent wedge of cheese Pip had ever smelled. The trouble was getting there.

Pip tried the obvious thing first: he just started running. Left, then right, then left again, down a corridor that ended in a wall. Back up. Down another one — also a wall. After ten minutes he found himself, panting, exactly where he had started.

Pip: “I've been everywhere and gotten nowhere! There are hundreds of paths. Do I really have to try every single one?”

Tess: “That would take all day, Pip. There has to be a tidier way to search.”

Professor Hoot glided down onto a hedge, looking very pleased, the way he always did right before a lesson.

Professor Hoot: “What you two need is a plan for exploring. Not a lucky guess — a method. The very same method your family's map app uses to find the fastest road home, and the same one a video-game character uses to chase you across a level. It's called search, or pathfinding.”

Spread out, one step at a time

Hoot hopped down and drew a glowing line on the dirt from Pip's paws to the nearest crossroads. “Stop thinking about the whole maze,” he said. “Think about one step. From where you stand right now, which places can you reach in a single step?”

Pip looked. Two corridors opened up — one north, one east. “I can go here, or here,” he said.

Professor Hoot: “Good. Mark both of those as ‘one step away.’ Now from each of those spots, where can you reach next? Mark everything new as ‘two steps away.’ Keep spreading outward like a ripple in a pond. Never step onto a spot you've already marked — you've been there. The very first time your ripple touches the cheese, follow the marks backward, and you will be holding the shortest path.”

Pip's whiskers twitched with delight. He wasn't guessing anymore. He was flooding the maze with little numbers, and the numbers were doing the thinking for him.

Tess: “So we explore the close places first, then the farther ones. Slow… but it can't possibly miss the shortest way.”

Professor Hoot: “Exactly, Tess. That careful, even ripple has a name — breadth-first search. And there's a cleverer cousin that runs straight toward the cheese. Let me show you both.”

🔍 How It Really Works

First, a computer turns the world into a graph. That's just a set of places — called nodes — joined by paths. Each open square in the maze is a node; if two squares are next to each other with no wall between, there's a path connecting them. A road map is a graph too: intersections are nodes, streets are the paths.

Search explores this graph outward from the start. The plain, careful version is breadth-first search. It works exactly like Pip's ripple: it visits every node one step away, then every node two steps away, then three, and so on. Because it always finishes the nearer ring before touching a farther one, the moment it reaches the goal it has found the shortest path — guaranteed. The catch is it spreads in every direction, even away from the goal, so it can do a lot of wasted exploring.

The smarter version is called A* (say “A-star”). It still spreads out, but it adds a heuristic — an educated guess of how far the goal still is, like “the cheese smells stronger this way.” Instead of treating all directions equally, A* always explores the spot whose steps-so-far plus smell-guess is smallest. That makes it lean toward the goal and skip most of the wandering, so it finds the same shortest path much faster.

One rule keeps any search honest: it must remember where it has already been. Cross a spot off the list once you've visited it. Forget to do that, and the search will happily walk in circles forever.

Pip followed the ripple, square by square, until a number landed right beside the cheese. Then he read the numbers backward — 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0 — and that trail of shrinking numbers was the exact shortest path home. He hadn't tried hundreds of routes. He'd tried each square exactly once.

✏️ Try It Yourself: Number the Maze

Draw a grid maze on paper — about 6 by 6 squares. Color in some squares as walls. Put a mouse in one corner and a piece of cheese somewhere far away.

  1. Write 0 in the mouse's square.
  2. Find every open square touching a 0 and write 1 in it. Then every open square touching a 1 gets a 2, and so on. Spread out one ring at a time. Never overwrite a number you've already written — that's how you remember where you've been.
  3. When the cheese's square gets a number, stop. Walk backward from it — …4, 3, 2, 1, 0 — and trace the shortest path! That numbering is breadth-first search.

Now try the fast cousin: at each step, peek at which open square is closest to the cheese as the crow flies and explore that one first. Notice how the “smell of cheese” pulls you toward the goal and saves you from numbering half the maze. That's the heuristic that powers A*.

⚠️ Watch Out

If a search forgets where it has already been, it can loop forever — walking from A to B to A to B and never noticing. Always cross off visited spots.

And a heuristic is only a guess. A bad guess — say, “the cheese is always north” when it's really east — can send A* charging the wrong way and make it slower than the plain ripple. Good search needs an honest hint. Remember too: more places and more paths mean more to explore, so a giant map (a whole city, a huge game world) really needs the clever, goal-seeking kind of search.

📖 Word Bank
Graph
A set of places joined by paths. Turning a maze or a map into a graph is the first thing a pathfinder does.
Node
One place in the graph — an open maze square, or an intersection on a map.
Search
Exploring a graph outward from the start to find a route to the goal, while remembering where you've already been.
Heuristic
An educated guess of how far the goal still is — like “the cheese smells stronger this way.” It steers a search toward the goal.
Shortest path
The route with the fewest steps from start to goal. Breadth-first search and A* are both guaranteed to find it.
💡 Big Idea

You don't have to try every path to find the best one — you just spread out carefully, remember where you've been, and let a good guess pull you toward the goal.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • A pathfinder turns the world into a graph of nodes joined by paths.
  • Breadth-first search spreads out one ring at a time and always finds the shortest path.
  • A* adds a heuristic — a smell-of-cheese guess — so it heads straight for the goal and finds the path faster.
  • A search must remember where it's been, or it loops forever — and a bad guess can lead it astray.
