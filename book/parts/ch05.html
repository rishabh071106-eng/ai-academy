Class Five

Nearest Neighbors

Birds of a feather — how a machine guesses what something is by looking at who it's most like.

Something was scratching at the Academy door. When Professor Hoot swung it open, in waddled a creature that no one — not one single animal in the whole classroom — had ever seen before. It was round and soft, a little bigger than a teacup. It had a tiny beak and whiskers. It made a sound halfway between a chirp and a squeak.

The room went quiet.

Mango: “What is it?”

Professor Hoot: “That, Mango, is exactly the question. There is no sign on its back. It came with no instructions. So — how shall we figure out what it is?”

Pip the mouse, who always noticed the small things, crept closer. She didn't try to make a rule. She just looked, very carefully, at the new creature, and then at everyone else in the room.

Pip: “It's small, like me. But it has a beak, like Coco. Maybe… maybe we shouldn't guess from nothing. Maybe we should find out who it's most like.”

Coco the parrot: “Birds of a feather flock together! Birds of a feather flock together!”

Professor Hoot: “Coco, you clever bird, you've just named the whole lesson.”

Let the neighbors vote

Here is what the class did. They picked a few simple things they could measure about any animal — its size, the sound it made, and what it liked to eat. Then they lined up the new creature next to the animals it resembled most.

By size, it sat near Pip and the other small ones. By sound, it landed somewhere between Coco's chirps and a mouse's squeak. By diet, it nibbled seeds — exactly like the birds. The class gathered the five animals it was most similar to overall and simply counted them up.

Pip: “Of its five closest matches… three are birds, two are mice. Birds win the vote. I think our mystery friend is a kind of bird.”

Professor Hoot: “No rules. No long list of questions. You just found its nearest neighbors and let them vote. Class, you've discovered one of the friendliest algorithms in all of AI.”

🔍 How It Really Works

This is the k-Nearest Neighbors algorithm, or k-NN for short. It works like a map. Every example you already know gets plotted as a dot, using its features as coordinates — size along one axis, sound along another. Similar animals end up sitting close together, so the birds form one little crowd and the mice form another.

When a mystery dot arrives, you measure the distance from it to every known dot — just how far apart they are on the map. You find the k closest dots (its nearest neighbors), look at their labels, and take a majority vote. Whatever most of the neighbors are, that's your guess.

That little letter k is the number of neighbors you ask. With k = 1 you trust the single closest dot — fast, but jumpy. With k = 5 you ask five and go with the crowd, which is steadier. People usually pick an odd number for k so the vote can't end in a tie.

Here's the surprising part: k-NN doesn't really train. There's no studying, no model being built. It simply remembers every example and, when a new one shows up, compares it to all of them on the spot. It's learning by remembering.

Notice how lazy this algorithm is, in the best possible way. The decision tree from last class worked hard up front, building its questions. k-NN does almost nothing until you ask it something — then it springs to life, measures every distance, and votes. It keeps the whole crowd of examples in its pocket and pulls them out only when it's needed.

✏️ Try It Yourself: The Two-Feature Map

You'll need a big sheet of paper, a ruler, and a handful of classmates or family members.

  1. Draw two lines for axes. Label the bottom one height and the side one shoe size.
  2. For each person you know, put a dot where their height and shoe size meet. Color kids' dots blue and grown-ups' dots green.
  3. Now think of a new person and plot their dot — but don't write the color yet.
  4. Find the three closest dots (k = 3) using your ruler. Are most of them blue or green? That's your prediction: kid or grown-up.

Check if you were right. You just ran k-NN by hand, ruler and all.

⚠️ Watch Out

Choosing k is a balancing act. Pick k too small — say k = 1 — and a single weird neighbor can fool you. One stray bird that wandered into the mouse crowd, and your guess goes wrong. Pick k too big and you drown out the local pattern: if you ask everyone in the room, the most common animal always wins, even when the answer is sitting right next door.

There's one more trap. Distance is fussy about numbers. If one feature is measured in huge values (say weight in grams: 5, 200, 4000) and another in small ones (number of legs: 2 or 4), the big-number feature bullies the measurement and the small one barely counts. The fix is scaling — shrinking every feature to a similar range first, so each one gets a fair vote.

📖 Word Bank
Nearest Neighbors
The known examples that sit closest to a new one on the feature map. To classify the new one, you look at them.
Distance
How far apart two examples are when you plot them by their features. Smaller distance means more similar.
k
The number of neighbors you ask. An odd k (like 3 or 5) keeps the vote from ending in a tie.
Majority Vote
Letting the neighbors decide: whichever label most of them have becomes the prediction.
Classification
Putting a new thing into the right group — bird or mouse, kid or grown-up.
💡 Big Idea

To guess what something is, you don't always need a rule — sometimes you just need to find who it's most like, and let the neighbors vote.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • k-Nearest Neighbors classifies a new thing by finding the known examples most similar to it.
  • Plot examples by their features, measure distance, and take a majority vote of the k closest.
  • The letter k matters: too small is jumpy, too big drowns out the local pattern — and odd numbers avoid ties.
  • k-NN never really trains — it just remembers everything and compares when asked.
