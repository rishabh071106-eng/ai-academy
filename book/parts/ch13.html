Class Thirteen

Language Models

How a chatbot writes a sentence — by playing the world's biggest guess-the-next-word game.

Coco the parrot had picked up a brand-new habit, and it was driving everyone slightly bananas. She had started finishing other people's sentences.

Mango: “Hey Coco, can you pass me the —”

Coco: “— BANANA! Pass me the banana!”

Pip: “Once upon a —”

Coco: “— TIME! Once upon a time!”

Every time, she guessed the next word before the speaker even got there. And the strange thing was — she was usually right.

Mango: “Professor, how does she do that? Is she reading our minds?”

Professor Hoot: “No mind-reading at all. Coco has heard so many sentences in her life that she's learned which words tend to come next. After ‘Once upon a…’, the word ‘time’ is almost always next. She isn't remembering one exact story — she's noticed a pattern across thousands of them.”

Mango's eyes went wide. He had used a chatbot at home — typed a question into a glowing box and watched it write back a whole, sensible answer. “Wait,” he said slowly. “Is that how the chatbots work too?”

Professor Hoot: “That, Mango, is exactly the secret. A chatbot is a giant, supercharged Coco. We call it a language model.”

The world's biggest guess-the-next-word game

Here is the trick that powers every chatbot you have ever used. A language model is trained on a colossal amount of text — books, websites, conversations, more words than you could read in a thousand lifetimes. From all that reading it learns just one skill, practiced billions of times: given the words so far, guess the word that comes next.

That sounds too simple to write a poem or answer a question. But watch what happens when you do it over and over. You guess one word. You add it to the sentence. Then you look at the new, slightly longer sentence and guess the next word. Then the next. String enough guesses together and out comes a full sentence — then a paragraph, then a whole story.

Coco: “So I'm not copying a story. I'm building one, one word at a time!”

Professor Hoot: “Precisely. And the very best part — it isn't looking up the answer in some hidden book. There is no book. It is predicting likely words, and likely words, strung together, sound an awful lot like knowing.”

🔍 How It Really Works

First, the text gets chopped into small pieces called tokens. A token is roughly a word or a chunk of a word — “banana” might be one token, while “unbelievable” could be split into “un”, “believ”, and “able”. The model only ever thinks in tokens.

During training, the model reads mountains of text and plays a guessing drill: it sees some tokens, tries to predict the next one, checks the real answer, and nudges itself to do better. Repeat that billions of times and it learns probabilities — given everything that came before, which token is likely to come next? After “The sky is”, the token “blue” gets a high probability; “spaghetti” gets a tiny one.

To write, it works one token at a time: predict a token, stick it on the end, then feed the longer text back in and predict again. This loop is called autoregression — each new word depends on all the words before it (building on the sequences and memory from Class 12). The text you type to start it off is called the prompt.

One honest truth: its “knowledge” is just patterns soaked up from its training text, not facts looked up in a database. To keep track of which earlier words matter most, it uses a clever trick called attention — and that is the star of the very next class.

“There's one more delicious detail,” said Professor Hoot. “The model doesn't have to grab the top word every single time. If it always did, it would be a bit boring and repeat itself. So it sometimes picks one of the other likely words — which is why a chatbot can tell you a different story every time you ask. A pinch of surprise makes it feel creative.”

Mango: “So when it writes me a story about a dragon, it's just… guessing dragon-ish words in a dragon-ish order?”

Professor Hoot: “Word by word by word. Magic? Maybe. But now you know the trick behind the curtain.”

✏️ Try It Yourself: Be a Language Model

You can play the next-word game with your own brain — no computer needed.

  1. Phone-keyboard version (with a trusted adult): open a texting app and type a starter word, like “I.” Now only ever tap the middle suggested word above the keyboard, again and again. Watch a wobbly, silly sentence build itself. That keyboard is a tiny language model!
  2. Group version: sit in a circle. The first person says a starter word (“Yesterday”). Each person in turn adds the single most predictable next word — the most ordinary, boring, expected word they can think of. Go around until someone ends the sentence.
  3. Now play it again, but let each person add a slightly surprising word instead. Compare the two sentences. One sounds plain; the other gets wonderfully weird. That's the “pinch of surprise” dial inside a chatbot!

You just felt how prediction, one word at a time, turns into language.

⚠️ Watch Out

Because a language model picks words that sound likely, it can sometimes write something completely wrong with total confidence — a fake fact, a made-up book, a wrong date — all in a calm, sure voice. People call this a hallucination. The model isn't lying; it simply doesn't truly know whether its words are true. It only knows they sound right. So always double-check anything important — a name, a number, a fact for homework — with a trustworthy source. (We'll dig into staying safe in the chapters on bias and safety.)

📖 Word Bank
Language model
An AI trained on huge amounts of text that writes by predicting the next word over and over. The big ones are called large language models, or LLMs — the engines inside chatbots.
Token
A small piece of text — roughly a word or part of a word. A model reads and writes in tokens, not letters.
Prompt
The text you give a model to start it off. A good prompt sets up a good answer.
Probability
How likely something is. The model gives every possible next token a probability and usually picks a likely one.
Hallucination
When a model states something false but sounds confident, because it predicted likely-sounding words instead of checking a fact.
💡 Big Idea

A chatbot doesn't look up answers — it writes them one word at a time, always guessing what's likely to come next.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • A language model learns from mountains of text to predict the next token.
  • It writes by guessing a word, adding it, and guessing again — that loop is autoregression.
  • Its “knowledge” is patterns from training text, not facts in a database — so it can hallucinate.
  • The text you give it to start is the prompt, and a little randomness lets it be creative.
