Class Seven

Linear Regression

The Line Into Tomorrow — how a machine predicts a number by drawing the best straight line through your data.

The Academy was having a contest, and it was a slow, sunny, patient kind of contest — exactly the sort Tess the tortoise loved. Every student had planted a sunflower seed in a paper cup, and the rule was simple: grow the tallest sunflower by the end of summer. But Professor Hoot added one twist. Each Friday, everyone had to measure their plant and write down how tall it was.

Bolt the rabbit measured his once, decided it was “basically a tree already,” and never measured again. Tess did something else. Week after week, she pressed her ruler against the stem and wrote a careful little number in her notebook.

Pip: “Two centimeters in Week 1… five in Week 2… eight in Week 3… Tess, your numbers are so neat.”

By Week 5, Tess had a column of numbers. She drew two lines on a fresh page — one going up the side for height, one going across the bottom for week — and placed a dot for each measurement. Then she leaned back and blinked.

Tess: “Look. The dots… they almost make a straight line.”

They did. The dots climbed upward in a tidy, slanted row, like steps on a gentle staircase. Not perfectly straight — one dot sat a hair high, another a hair low — but if you squinted, a single straight line could slide right through the middle of all of them.

Mango: “So what? It's a nice line. Can it do anything?”

Tess smiled her slow tortoise smile. She laid her ruler along the dots, drew the line, and kept drawing it — past her last dot, out into the empty future part of the chart, all the way to Week 8.

Tess: “If the dots keep following this line, then in Week 8 my sunflower should be about… twenty centimeters tall. That's my prediction.”

Predicting a number, not a category

Professor Hoot ruffled his feathers, delighted. “Class, remember when Ziggy sorted fruit into crates? She put each thing into a category — red, yellow, green. That's called classification. But Tess isn't sorting anything. She's guessing a number — how many centimeters tall. Guessing a number from a pattern has its own name. It's called regression.”

Three weeks later, the class gathered around Tess's cup with a ruler, holding their breath. Week 8. The sunflower stood tall and proud. Pip measured carefully: twenty-one centimeters. Tess had guessed twenty. Close — wonderfully, impressively close — using nothing but a notebook, a ruler, and a straight line.

🔍 How It Really Works

Tess just did linear regression — one of the most useful tools in all of machine learning. Here's the honest version.

Each measurement becomes a dot on a chart. One thing you know goes across the bottom (the input — the week), and the thing you want to predict goes up the side (the number — the height). Five measurements, five dots.

Now you draw a straight line through them. But not just any line — you want the one that comes closest to all the dots at once. For each dot, measure the gap between the dot and the line; that gap is called the error. A computer tries the line that makes the total error as small as possible. That special line has a name: the line of best fit.

Once you have the line, predicting is easy. Pick a new input — say, Week 8 — find it along the bottom, go up to the line, and read off the height. That's your prediction.

One gentle bit of math: a straight line has a slope (how steep it is — how many centimeters of growth per week) and a starting point (where it begins on the side). Learning the line just means finding the best slope and starting point. That's the whole trick. Classification picks a category; regression predicts a number.

That's why this idea shows up everywhere. The same straight-line trick predicts how much a house will cost from its size, how long a trip will take from its distance, or how much a plant drinks from how hot the day is. Whenever you have dots that lean in a direction, a line can carry the pattern forward into tomorrow.

✏️ Try It Yourself: Draw the Line Into Tomorrow

Pick something real that changes a little each day and track it:

  1. Choose your number — a sprouting bean's height, the coins in your savings jar, or the temperature at the same time each afternoon.
  2. Measure it once a day for at least five days and write down each number.
  3. Draw two lines on paper: day across the bottom, your number up the side. Place one dot per day.
  4. Lay a ruler down and draw the straight line that passes as close to all the dots as you can — about as many dots above it as below.
  5. Keep the line going one day past your last dot. Where it lands is your prediction. Write it down.
  6. Tomorrow, measure for real. How close were you?

Congratulations — you just fit a line of best fit by eye, exactly like Tess.

⚠️ Watch Out

A line will happily keep going forever — but the real world doesn't. Tess's line says her sunflower grows about 2 cm every week. Follow that line far enough and it promises a sunflower taller than the school, then taller than a mountain, then poking the Moon. Of course that's silly. Real sunflowers slow down and stop.

Stretching a line way past your data is called extrapolation, and the farther you go, the less you can trust it. Predicting next week? Pretty safe. Predicting next year? Don't bet your lunch on it. And one more thing: not every pattern is a straight line — some dots curve. If your dots don't lean in a tidy slant, a straight line is the wrong tool.

📖 Word Bank
Regression
Predicting a number from a pattern (like height in cm) — as opposed to classification, which picks a category.
Line of best fit
The single straight line that passes as close as possible to all your dots at once.
Error
The gap between a dot and the line. The best line makes the total error as small as it can be.
Slope
How steep the line is — how much the number changes for each step along the bottom (here, centimeters of growth per week).
Prediction
The answer you read off the line at a new input you haven't measured yet.
💡 Big Idea

If your dots lean one way, the best straight line through them can carry the pattern forward — and turn yesterday's measurements into tomorrow's guess.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • Regression predicts a number; classification picks a category.
  • Each example is a dot; the line of best fit comes as close to all of them as possible.
  • “Closest” means making the total error — the gaps from dots to line — as small as it can be.
  • You predict by reading the line at a new input — but stretch it too far and the prediction stops being trustworthy.
