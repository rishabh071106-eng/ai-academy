Class Eleven

Computer Vision

How can a machine "see," when all it ever gets is a giant grid of numbers?

Professor Hoot held up a photograph of a fluffy gray cat. “Tell me,” he said, “what is this?” The whole class shouted at once: “A cat!” Easy. Pip the mouse, who notices things nobody else does, smiled — she'd already counted its whiskers.

“Good,” said the owl. “Now watch.” He tapped the photo, and on the big screen it began to zoom. Closer. Closer. The cat's soft ear blurred into a fuzzy patch. The patch broke into blobs. The blobs broke into… tiny colored squares. Zoomed all the way in, the cat was gone. There was nothing left but a grid of little squares, each one a single flat color.

Professor Hoot: “This, class, is what a computer actually sees. Not a cat. Not even a picture. Just a grid of dots — and every dot is only a number telling how bright it is.”

A new friend had flown in for this class: Skye, a sharp-eyed eagle who can spot a fish from the top of a tree. Skye squinted at the screen.

Skye: “So a picture is secretly a spreadsheet? Every little dot is a number — a 0 for black, a 9 for bright white, and everything in between?”

Professor Hoot: “Exactly so. Each tiny square is called a pixel. A phone photo has millions of them. To the computer, the whole image is just a colossal grid of numbers. So here is our puzzle…” He looked straight at Pip. “If all the machine gets is numbers, how on earth does it ever say cat?”

Build the big thing from small parts

Pip went very quiet, the way she does when she's thinking hard. “When I look at a cat,” she said softly, “I don't see ‘a cat' all at once. First I notice little things. A pointy edge here. A curved line there. Then I notice those edges make a triangle — an ear. Two round shapes — eyes. A row of straight lines — whiskers. And only then, after I've spotted all the little parts, my brain says: cat!”

Professor Hoot: “Pip, you have just described how a machine learns to see. It does not try to recognize a whole cat in one giant leap. It starts with the simplest parts and builds up.”

Edges first. Then edges joined into small shapes — a corner, a curve. Then shapes joined into bigger parts — an ear, an eye, a nose. Then all the parts together — a face, a body, a whole cat. Small things stacked into bigger things, layer by layer. Skye nodded slowly. “That's how I hunt, too. First I spot a flash of movement. Then a fin. Then — fish!”

🔍 How It Really Works

To a computer, an image is a grid of pixels, and each pixel is just a number for its brightness (a color pixel is three numbers — red, green, blue). That's the whole input: rows and rows of numbers.

To find patterns in those numbers, the machine uses a special kind of neural network — the same idea from Classes 9 and 10, but built for grids. It's called a convolutional neural network, or CNN. Its trick is a little filter (also called a feature detector): a tiny window, maybe 3×3 pixels, that slides across the whole image, one patch at a time, lighting up wherever it finds the pattern it's looking for. One filter might fire on vertical edges; another on a patch of green; another on a corner.

Here's the beautiful part. The early layers of filters find simple features — edges, corners, blobs of color. The next layer combines those into shapes — a circle, a curve, a stripe. Deeper layers combine those into parts — an eye, an ear, a wheel. And the final layers combine the parts into whole objects — a cat, a car, a face. Nobody tells the network what an edge or an ear is; it learns the useful filters from thousands of example pictures. Stacking many layers is exactly why these deep networks are so astonishingly good at vision.

Skye: “So the filters are like my eyes' first instinct — they hunt for the small clues — and the deeper layers are my brain putting the clues together.”

Professor Hoot: “A fine way to picture it. And notice why Pip is so good at this. She is patient with small parts. A machine that sees must be patient with small parts too — because the small parts are all it has.”

✏️ Try It Yourself: Become a Pixel Artist

Turn a picture into data the way a computer does it. You'll need graph paper (or draw a grid of squares — 10×10 is great) and a pencil.

  1. Draw something simple inside the grid by filling whole squares: a heart, a smiley, the letter of your name. Only fill a square if your shape covers most of it.
  2. Now write a number in every square: 0 for an empty (white) square, 9 for a fully filled (black) square, and something in between (like 4 or 5) for squares you only shaded a little.
  3. You just turned a picture into a grid of numbers — the exact thing a computer "sees."
  4. Trade grids with a friend. Read only the numbers, not the drawing, and try to color in each square by its number. Can you decode their picture?

If your friend can rebuild your drawing from numbers alone, you've proved that a picture really is just data.

⚠️ Watch Out

Because a vision AI only ever sees numbers, it can be fooled in ways that would never trick you. Researchers have changed a few pixels in a photo — so few that a human sees no difference at all — and watched a confident AI suddenly call a panda a gibbon. These sneaky tricks are called adversarial examples.

And remember: a CNN only recognizes things similar to what it was trained on. Show it a cat in a silly costume, a cat upside down, or a kind of cat it never studied, and it may be completely stumped. It learned patterns from its examples — it doesn't truly "understand" cats the way you do.

📖 Word Bank
Pixel
One tiny dot of a picture. To a computer it's just a number (or three) saying how bright and what color that dot is.
Computer vision
The field of teaching machines to make sense of images and video — to find and recognize what's in a picture made of pixels.
Filter (feature detector)
A small window that slides across the image hunting for one simple pattern, like an edge or a corner, lighting up wherever it finds it.
Convolutional neural network (CNN)
A neural network built for grids of pixels. It slides many filters over an image and stacks them in layers to recognize objects.
Feature
A useful clue the network finds — from simple ones (edges) in early layers to complex ones (an eye, a whole face) in deeper layers.
💡 Big Idea

A machine doesn't see a cat — it sees a grid of numbers, and learns to build the cat out of edges, then shapes, then parts, one layer at a time.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • To a computer, every picture is just a grid of pixels — a giant grid of numbers.
  • A CNN slides little filters across the image to find simple patterns like edges.
  • Early layers find simple features; deeper layers combine them into shapes, parts, and whole objects.
  • Vision AI can be fooled by a few changed pixels, and only knows things like what it was trained on.
