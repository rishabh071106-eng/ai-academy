Class Eight

Overfitting

Memorizing isn't learning — the difference between truly understanding an idea and just remembering the answers.

It was test week at the Academy, and the whole class was nervous — all except Bolt the rabbit, who was bouncing with confidence. Professor Hoot had handed out a practice quiz the day before: five questions, with the answers printed neatly on the back. “Study these well,” the owl had said, “and tomorrow's real test will feel friendly.”

Bolt took one look at the practice answers and grinned. He didn't bother to understand any of them. Instead, he did what he was fastest at: he memorized. Question one, answer “forty-two.” Question two, answer “the river.” By bedtime he could recite all five answers backward, forward, and in his sleep.

Bolt: “I've got this! I know every single answer by heart.”

Tess the tortoise studied differently — and slower, of course. She barely looked at the printed answers. Instead, she kept asking why each one was right. “If the answer is the river, what's the idea behind it? When would the answer be the lake instead?” She wasn't collecting answers. She was collecting the reasons.

Tess: “I might be slow, Bolt, but I'd rather understand five questions than memorize five answers.”

The next morning, Professor Hoot passed out the real test — and flipped the whole class upside down. The questions were brand new. Same ideas, completely different wording. Not one of them matched the practice quiz.

Bolt's ears drooped. His memorized answers were useless — they fit the old questions perfectly and the new ones not at all. He'd scored a glorious 100% on the practice and now sat staring at a test he couldn't touch. Tess, meanwhile, read each new question, recognized the underlying idea, and answered it calmly. She aced the test she had never seen.

Professor Hoot: “Bolt, you didn't learn the material. You memorized it. In my world, we have a name for that mistake. We call it overfitting.”

Great on the old, useless on the new

Here is the trap, and it's a sneaky one. Bolt looked like the best student in the room — right up until the questions changed. A model that has overfit is exactly like Bolt: it performs brilliantly on the examples it studied, and then falls apart the moment it meets something new. The whole point of learning is to handle the new. Memorizing the old gets you nowhere.

🔍 How It Really Works

When we train a model, we feed it a pile of examples called the training set. The model's goal is not really to ace those examples — it's to learn the pattern underneath them so it can handle new examples it has never seen. That skill has a name: generalization.

Overfitting happens when a model hugs the training data too closely. It memorizes not just the real pattern but also every random bump, typo, and quirk — the noise. Like Bolt, it scores almost perfectly on what it has seen and then flops on anything new, because it learned the answers instead of the idea.

The opposite mistake is underfitting: a model so simple it misses the real pattern entirely. That's like a student who only learned “the answer is probably a number” — too vague to be right about anything. The goal is the sweet spot in the middle: complex enough to catch the true pattern, simple enough to ignore the noise.

So how do we catch overfitting honestly, before it's too late? We hide some data. We split our examples into a training set (the model studies these) and a separate test set (the model never sees these while learning). Then we check the score on the test set — the questions it couldn't have memorized. That's the real test, exactly like Hoot's. And here's a surprising truth scientists rely on every day: a simpler model often generalizes better than a fancy, complicated one. Back in Class Four we warned that a decision tree grown too big starts memorizing instead of learning — that was overfitting, and now you know its full name.

Look closely at that wiggly purple line. It passes through every dot perfectly — a flawless 100% on the dots it was given. But it's a fake. Drop a brand-new dot anywhere near the middle and the wiggly line will guess something silly, because it was busy hugging old dots instead of learning the gentle upward trend. The calm green curve misses a dot or two on purpose, and that's exactly why it will do better on everything that comes next.

✏️ Try It Yourself: The Multiplication Test

This experiment shows the difference between memorizing and understanding — using just a pencil and a friend.

  1. Be the "memorizing model." Memorize these five facts and nothing else: 6×7=42, 8×4=32, 9×3=27, 7×5=35, 6×6=36. Don't think about how multiplication works — just chant the five answers until you know them cold.
  2. Now be the "understanding model." On a different day, learn what multiplication actually means: 6×7 is six groups of seven, which you can count up or add.
  3. Take the real test. Have your friend ask you different facts you never memorized — 8×6, 7×9, 4×5. The memorizing version is stuck. The understanding version can work out every single one.

Which "model" generalized? The one that learned the idea, not the answers. That's the whole lesson of overfitting in one pencil-and-paper test.

⚠️ Watch Out

If a model scores a perfect 100% on its training data, do not cheer — get suspicious. A perfect training score is usually a red flag, not a trophy. It often means the model memorized the examples (noise and all) instead of learning the pattern. The only honest scoreboard is a test set the model has never, ever seen. If it does well on fresh, unseen questions — then you can celebrate.

📖 Word Bank
Overfitting
When a model fits its training examples too closely — memorizing even the random noise — so it does great on what it has seen and badly on anything new.
Underfitting
The opposite mistake: a model too simple to catch the real pattern, so it does poorly on everything, old and new alike.
Training set
The examples a model studies and learns from — its practice quiz.
Test set
A separate batch of examples the model never sees while learning, kept aside to check honestly whether it really understood — its real test.
Generalization
The goal of all learning: handling new, unseen examples correctly, not just the ones you studied.
💡 Big Idea

Learning isn't remembering the answers — it's understanding the idea well enough to answer questions you've never seen before.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • Overfitting means fitting the training data so closely you memorize its noise and fail on anything new.
  • Underfitting is the opposite — too simple to catch the pattern at all. The goal is the sweet spot in between.
  • We split data into a training set and a separate test set so we can check learning honestly.
  • A perfect training score is a red flag; simpler models often generalize better.
