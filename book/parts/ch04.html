Class Four

Decision Trees

The Question Game — how a machine makes a decision by asking the smartest question first.

Ziggy the zebra had a problem, and it was a fruity one. Professor Hoot had dumped an enormous basket on the classroom floor — apples, bananas, green pears, cherries, lemons, a single lonely blueberry — and asked the class to sort every piece into the right crate. There were three crates: red things, yellow things, and green things.

“Easy!” said Bolt the rabbit, and he began flinging fruit at top speed. A banana sailed into the red crate. A cherry bounced into the green one. Within thirty seconds he had made a magnificent, colorful mess.

Professor Hoot: “Bolt, you sorted fast. But did you sort well?”

Ziggy did something different. She didn't touch a single fruit yet. Instead, she stood very still and thought about the best question to ask first.

Ziggy: “If I ask, ‘Is it a cherry?’, that only helps with the cherries. But if I ask, ‘Is it red?’ — that splits the whole basket almost in half in one go!”

And that was the secret. Ziggy held up each fruit and asked one yes-or-no question at a time. Is it red? If yes, it went toward the red crate. If no, she asked a second question: Is it yellow? Yes meant the yellow crate; no meant green. Two small questions, and every single fruit landed in exactly the right place.

Mango: “You built a little tree of questions in your head!”

Professor Hoot: “She certainly did. And that, class, is one of the oldest and most useful tricks in all of artificial intelligence. It's called a decision tree.”

Why the first question matters most

Here's the part Ziggy understood and Bolt didn't: not all questions are equally good. A great question is one that splits a messy pile into neater piles. “Is it red?” is a fantastic first question because it instantly separates apples and cherries from everything else. “Is it a blueberry?” is a terrible first question, because no matter the answer, you've barely sorted anything — there was only one blueberry!

A decision tree always tries to ask the most powerful question first, then the next most powerful, and so on, until every pile is tidy.

🔍 How It Really Works

A real decision tree learns from examples. Imagine you give a computer a list of fruits, each one described by its features — color, size, whether it's bumpy — along with the right answer (its name).

The computer tests every possible question and measures how much each one tidies up the mess. Scientists have a name for the mess: they call it impurity. A pile with apples and bananas and pears all jumbled together has high impurity. A pile that's all apples has zero impurity — it's perfectly pure.

The tree picks the question that reduces impurity the most, splits the data on it, and then repeats the whole process on each new pile. It keeps going until each pile is pure (or close enough). That's it — that's the whole algorithm. No magic, just smart questions in a smart order.

The wonderful thing about a decision tree is that you can read it. If the machine sorts a fruit into the green crate, you can ask why? and it will tell you: “Because it was not red, and it was not yellow.” That makes decision trees one of the most honest kinds of AI — they never hide their reasoning. Doctors, banks, and scientists love them for exactly that reason.

✏️ Try It Yourself: Guess the Animal

Grab a friend and play the classic 20 Questions game — but pay attention to your strategy. Your friend thinks of an animal. You may only ask yes-or-no questions.

  1. Your first question should split the animal kingdom roughly in half. Good first questions: “Does it live in water?” or “Is it bigger than a dog?” Bad first question: “Is it a hamster?”
  2. After each answer, pick the next question that splits what's left in half.
  3. Count your questions. Can you guess any animal in 7 questions or fewer?

Congratulations — you just were a decision tree. Every good question you asked reduced the impurity of the “pile” of possible animals.

⚠️ Watch Out

A decision tree can get too clever. If you let it keep asking questions forever, it will grow a giant tree with one tiny branch for every single example — basically memorizing the answers instead of learning the pattern. That's called overfitting, and it's such an important trap that it gets its own class later (Class 8). The fix is to stop the tree while it's still simple.

📖 Word Bank
Decision tree
A model that makes a decision by asking a series of yes-or-no questions, branching like a tree until it reaches an answer.
Feature
One describable fact about a thing — a fruit's color, size, or bumpiness. Features are the clues a model gets to use.
Impurity
How mixed-up a pile is. The tree's whole job is to ask questions that lower impurity until each pile is neat.
Leaf
The end of a branch, where the tree stops asking and gives its final answer.
💡 Big Idea

A smart decision isn't about asking many questions — it's about asking the right question first.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • A decision tree sorts things by asking yes-or-no questions in a clever order.
  • The best question is the one that tidies the mess the most.
  • Trees are easy to read — they can always explain their reasoning.
  • Let a tree grow too big and it starts memorizing instead of learning.
