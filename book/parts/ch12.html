Class Twelve

Memory & Sequences

Remembering what came before — how an AI predicts what comes next when the order matters.

Coco the parrot could not sit still when there was music in the room. The moment Professor Hoot tapped out a tune on the old classroom piano, her head began to bob and her feathers fluffed up with joy. Coco loved songs — and, more than that, she remembered them. Hum half a melody anywhere in the forest and Coco would finish it for you.

So Professor Hoot decided to set her a little test. He played four soft notes — low, high, low, higher — and then, right in the middle of the fifth, he stopped. His wing froze above the keys.

Professor Hoot: “Coco. What's the next note?”

Coco didn't even hesitate. She sang it — a bright, rising note — and it was exactly right. Tess the tortoise blinked, slowly, the way Tess does.

Tess: “How did you know? He never played that note.”

Coco: “I didn't know it. I guessed it — from the notes that came before. The song was climbing. So the next note climbs too.”

Professor Hoot ruffled with delight. He pressed the same five keys again, but this time in a jumbled order. It sounded like a sneeze. Nobody's head bobbed.

Professor Hoot: “Same notes, Coco. Why isn't it the same song?”

Coco: “Because the order changed! A song isn't just a bag of notes. It's notes in a line. Mix up the line and you've got a different song — or no song at all.”

The world is full of lines

Here is the big secret Coco understood in her feathery little heart: lots of things in the world come as sequences — ordered lines where the position of each item matters. A song is a sequence of notes. A sentence is a sequence of words. The weather is a sequence of days. Your morning is a sequence of steps (socks usually go on before shoes).

And to guess what comes next in any of these, you have to remember what came before. You need memory. Coco's gift for holding a sequence in her head is the very same trick that taught machines to finish our sentences and write little tunes of their own.

🔍 How It Really Works

A sequence is an ordered list where the position of each item matters: cat and act use the same letters, but they are not the same word. To predict the next item, a machine reads the sequence one item at a time, left to right.

The clever part is that, as it reads, it keeps a little pocket of memory — engineers call it the state. The state is a short summary of everything the machine has seen so far. At each step it does two things at once: it uses state + the new item to predict the next item, and it updates its state to fold the new item in. Then it loops. Read, remember, predict. Read, remember, predict.

Because it loops back on itself this way, this kind of model is called a Recurrent Neural Network, or RNN. (Remember the neural network from an earlier class? An RNN is one of those, wired so a piece of its output feeds back in as memory.) For years, RNNs were how computers handled language and music — reading a sentence word by word and guessing the next word, just like Coco guessing the next note.

But the memory is small, and it fades. Each new item nudges the old summary aside, so something from way back at the start of a long sequence can slip away before the machine reaches the end. Fixing that fading memory is exactly what the next big idea — attention — was invented to do.

Notice what the chain is doing. The first box reads Once and remembers it. The second box reads upon but also gets the memory of Once. The third reads a with the memory of Once upon tucked inside. By the time we reach the end, the machine isn't staring at a single lonely word — it's holding the whole little story so far. That's why it can confidently sing out “time.”

✏️ Try It Yourself: The Story Chain

This is a memory game you can play with one friend or a whole table of them.

  1. Start a sentence with two or three words, out loud: “Once upon a …” The next player must add exactly one word that fits.
  2. Keep going around. Each new player has to remember the whole sentence in order before adding their word. No writing it down!
  3. When someone forgets the order, or adds a word that doesn't fit what came before, the chain breaks and the round ends.

Try a number version too: 2, 4, 6, 8, ? — what comes next? Or red, orange, yellow, ? To answer, you used your memory of the earlier items to spot the pattern. You just were a sequence model. And notice how much harder it gets to remember the start once the sentence runs very long…

⚠️ Watch Out

Small memory forgets the beginning. Read this: “The parrot who lived in the tall green tree by the river that the woodpeckers loved … sang.” By the end you may have lost track of who was doing the singing! Simple RNNs have the same trouble: with only a little state, each new word crowds out the older ones, so facts from long ago fade before they're needed. This real weakness is exactly why smarter methods came next.

📖 Word Bank
Sequence
An ordered list of items where the position matters — a song, a sentence, a row of days. Shuffle the order and you change the meaning.
Memory (state)
A little pocket of information a model keeps as it reads, summarizing everything it has seen so far so it can use the past to guess the future.
Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)
A neural network that reads a sequence one item at a time and feeds part of its output back in as memory — looping so the past flows forward.
Prediction
The model's best guess at the next item in the sequence, made from its memory plus the item it just read.
💡 Big Idea

To guess what comes next, a machine has to remember what came before — and keep that memory in order.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • A sequence is an ordered line of items, and the order changes the meaning.
  • A sequence model reads one item at a time, keeping a small memory of what it has seen.
  • It uses that memory to predict the next item — these loop-back models are called RNNs.
  • Their memory fades, so long-ago things get forgotten — a problem waiting to be fixed.
