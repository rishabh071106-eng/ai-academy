Class Seventeen

Recommendation Systems

How an app seems to know the next video, song, or snack you'll love — before you've even tried it.

The forest had a delicious problem. Somewhere between the river and the old oak, hundreds of berry bushes grew — sweet ones, sour ones, fizzy purple ones, tiny pink ones that tasted like rain. There were far too many to ever try them all. A monkey could spend his whole life tasting berries and still miss the best one in the forest.

Mango: “There must be a thousand bushes out here! How am I supposed to know which berry to eat next? I'll be a very old monkey before I finish.”

Bolt, naturally, had a plan that involved speed.

Bolt: “Just taste them all, fast! I'll start at this end, you start at that end, we'll meet in the middle by sundown.”

But Mango noticed something while he sat thinking. His friend Ziggy loved the exact same berries he did — the fizzy purple ones, the sour blue ones. Every time Mango adored a berry, it turned out Ziggy had adored it too. Their tastes were practically twins.

Mango: “Ziggy! You and I always love the same berries. So if you found a new bush you loved, and I haven't tried it yet… I'd probably love it too, right?”

Ziggy: “The pink-rain berries by the waterfall. You haven't had those. I think about them every night.”

Mango sprinted to the waterfall, popped one in his mouth, and his eyes went wide. It was the best berry he'd ever tasted. He hadn't searched the whole forest. He'd simply borrowed the taste of someone who liked what he liked.

Professor Hoot: “Mango, you've just invented one of the cleverest tricks on the internet. Every time an app suggests a video, a song, or a snack, it is whispering the very thing you said: animals like you also liked this.”

Finding your taste twin

Here's the heart of it. Nobody can taste everything — not Mango, and not you, with millions of videos and songs in an app. So instead of searching everything, the trick is to find the few people whose tastes match yours, and let them point the way. If you and someone else loved the same ten songs, chances are good you'll love their eleventh.

This is the same idea as the neighbors trick from Class Five: to guess something about you, find the people closest to you and copy their answer. Back then we were finding nearest neighbors to label a picture. Here we're finding your nearest neighbors in taste to pick your next favorite thing.

🔍 How It Really Works

A recommendation system starts with a giant scoreboard of ratings: every like, thumbs-up, star, or "watched it twice" that lots and lots of people have left. Picture an enormous grid — one row per person, one column per item — with a heart in a cell when that person loved that item, and a blank where they haven't tried it.

The most famous method is called collaborative filtering, and it works in two steps. First it finds your taste twins — the people whose pattern of hearts looks most similar to yours (this is the nearest-neighbors idea from Class Five, measuring closeness). Then it looks at the items your twins loved that you haven't tried yet — your blank cells — and recommends those. It's "people like you also liked…", done with math.

There's a second flavor called content-based filtering. Instead of comparing people, it compares items: if you loved a fast, funny, purple-berry kind of song, it finds other fast, funny, purple-berry songs. And here's the magic part: every time you tap like, skip, or watch-to-the-end, you fill in another cell, so the system's guesses get sharper the more you use it.

Notice what the grid never needed: it never had to know why a berry was tasty. It didn't measure sweetness or color or fizz. It only watched who loved what, and let the patterns of agreement do the work. That's why these systems can recommend things even a human expert couldn't explain — they're matchmaking on pure behavior.

✏️ Try It Yourself: Find Your Taste Twin

You can run collaborative filtering by hand with a few friends and a pencil.

  1. Agree on five movies (or songs, or games) everyone knows. Make a grid: friends down the side, the five titles across the top.
  2. Everyone secretly rates each one from 1 to 5 stars. Fill in every cell. Then reveal the grids.
  3. Find your taste twin: the friend whose star ratings are closest to yours. (Add up the differences cell by cell — the smallest total wins.)
  4. Now ask your twin for one favorite movie that you've never seen. That's your recommendation. Go watch it.

Did it land? If it did, you just felt collaborative filtering work. If it didn't, you've also just discovered why apps need lots of people, not just one twin, to guess well.

⚠️ Watch Out

If a recommender only ever shows you more of what you already like, you can get trapped in a filter bubble — a cozy little world where you only see purple berries and forget that yellow ones even exist. You stop discovering new music, new ideas, new kinds of people. The fix is to leave room for surprise: try a bush no twin recommended.

Recommenders can also be gamed. If sneaky animals leave a pile of fake hearts on a bland berry, the system may push it to everyone. Fake likes, paid reviews, and bot accounts all bend the recommendations — which is exactly the kind of unfairness we met when we talked about bias.

📖 Word Bank
Recommendation system
Software that suggests the next item you might like — a video, song, or product — based on what you and others have enjoyed before.
Collaborative filtering
Finding people whose tastes are similar to yours and recommending the things they loved that you haven't tried yet.
Rating
A signal of how much someone liked something — a star, a like, a thumbs-up, or even "watched it to the end." Ratings are the fuel for a recommender.
Similarity
A measure of how close two people's (or two items') tastes are. The closer the match, the better the prediction.
Filter bubble
What happens when a recommender shows you only more of the same, so you stop discovering anything new.
💡 Big Idea

You don't have to taste every berry in the forest — you just have to find the friends who like what you like, and follow their hearts.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • A recommendation system guesses what you'll like next from a giant scoreboard of everyone's ratings.
  • Collaborative filtering finds your taste twins and suggests what they loved that you haven't tried — the neighbors trick from Class Five.
  • Content-based recommending compares the items instead, suggesting things similar to ones you already loved.
  • Beware the filter bubble — and remember fake likes can fool a recommender.
