Class Nine

Neural Networks

How to build a brain out of thousands of tiny, simple switches — none of them clever, all of them working together.

Professor Hoot pushed all the desks against the wall and clapped his wings. “Today,” he announced, “we are not going to read about a brain. We are going to become one. Everybody up. Line up in rows.”

The class shuffled into three neat rows facing the chalkboard. Bolt was in the front. Pip and Mango were in the middle. Coco stood alone at the very back.

Professor Hoot: “Each of you is now a tiny part of a brain. We call one of these tiny parts a neuron. And here is the most important rule of the whole day: your job is incredibly dumb.”

Mango: “Dumb? I came here to get smarter!”

Professor Hoot: “Patience. Here is your one and only job. Listen to the numbers that get shouted at you. Add them up. If the total is big enough to be exciting — shout to the row behind you. If it's too small, stay quiet. That's it. That's everything you do.”

Bolt frowned. “That doesn't sound like thinking at all.”

Professor Hoot: “One of you, alone, can barely do anything. But watch what rows of you can do together.”

The Professor wrote a secret drawing on a card — a scribbly creature with whiskers and pointy ears. Then he fed numbers about the drawing to the front row: “Pointy ears: a 9! Whiskers: an 8! A long flat beak: a 0!”

Bolt heard “pointy ears: 9” and a couple of other numbers. He added them up. The total felt exciting. So Bolt shouted a big number to the middle row. Next to him, an animal who only heard small, boring numbers stayed perfectly quiet.

The middle row didn't see the drawing at all. They only heard how loudly the front row was shouting. Pip listened — lots of front-row animals were yelling “ears!” and “whiskers!” at once. That felt exciting. So Pip shouted to the back.

Finally Coco, all alone at the back, added up everything she heard from the middle row. The total was huge. She took a breath and yelled the answer the whole network had been building toward:

Coco: “It's a CAT!”

The Professor flipped the card around. It was a cat. The room erupted.

Nobody in the room knew it was a cat

Here's the spooky part, and the Professor made sure everyone caught it. Bolt never knew it was a cat — he only knew “pointy ears: 9.” Pip never knew either — she only knew “the front row is yelling a lot.” Not one single animal understood the whole picture.

Professor Hoot: “And yet together you recognized a cat. Each of you did one tiny multiply-add-and-shout. Stacked into rows, those dumb little steps added up to something genuinely clever. That, class, is a neural network — a brain built out of math.”

🔍 How It Really Works

A real neuron takes in several numbers — its inputs. But it doesn't treat them all the same. Each input is multiplied by a weight, a number that says how much this input matters. A big weight means “pay close attention to this one.” A weight near zero means “ignore it.” The neuron multiplies every input by its weight, then adds all those products into one total.

Then comes the decision. The total is passed through a tiny step called an activation, which decides how strongly the neuron should “fire.” A common one is simple: if the total is above a threshold, fire; if not, stay quiet — exactly like the animals shouting or staying silent.

One neuron is almost useless. The power comes from stacking them into layers. The input layer takes the raw facts (the pixels of a drawing). One or more hidden layers in the middle combine those facts into bigger ideas (“this looks like an ear,” “this looks like a whisker”). The output layer gives the final answer (“cat” or “not cat”). Signals flow forward, row by row, from input to output.

So where is the network's “knowledge”? It lives entirely in the weights — those millions of little numbers. Learning means adjusting the weights until the answers come out right. (How a network tunes its own weights is the whole story of the next class.) And deep learning? That fancy phrase just means a network with many hidden layers stacked deep.

Mango raised a hand. “So if I want the network to recognize a dog instead of a cat… I just change the numbers on the lines?”

Professor Hoot: “Exactly right. Same animals, same rows, same dumb little jobs. You only change the weights — which signals get shouted loudly and which get hushed. Get the weights right and the very same network can spot a dog, read your handwriting, or steer a car.”

✏️ Try It Yourself: Be a Human Network

You need a handful of friends and a little open space. Line them up in two or three rows, all facing the same way.

  1. Give each person in the front row a secret number on a card. Tell them the rule: “If your number is bigger than 5, raise your hand.” That's their activation.
  2. Each person in the next row can't see the cards — they can only count the raised hands in front of them. Their rule: “If you can count 2 or more raised hands, you raise yours too.”
  3. Add one more row with the rule “Raise your hand if anyone in front of you did.” The last person raising a hand is the network's final decision.

Now change one front-row card and run it again. Watch a single tiny change ripple forward through the rows to flip the final answer. You just ran a feed-forward neural network made of people.

⚠️ Watch Out

A neural network is not a real brain, and it is not conscious. It doesn't feel, want, or understand anything. Under all the excitement it is just a big stack of the same dull step over and over: multiply, add, and decide whether to fire. When Coco shouted “CAT!” she had no idea what a cat is — she only had a very large number.

And don't be fooled by any single neuron. One on its own can barely tell big numbers from small ones. All the cleverness comes from many of them, wired together in layers.

📖 Word Bank
Neuron
One tiny unit in the network. It takes several numbers in, multiplies each by a weight, adds them up, and decides how strongly to fire.
Weight
A number on each connection that says how much an input matters. All of the network's knowledge is stored in its weights.
Layer
A row of neurons. Networks stack an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer; signals flow forward through them.
Activation
The little step that decides how strongly a neuron fires once its inputs are added up — like “shout if the total is exciting enough.”
Deep learning
Simply a neural network with many hidden layers stacked deep. More layers let it learn more complicated patterns.
💡 Big Idea

Thousands of tiny, dumb switches — each just adding numbers and deciding whether to fire — can together do something genuinely smart.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • A neuron does one simple job: weigh its inputs, add them up, and decide whether to fire.
  • Neurons are stacked in layers, and signals flow forward from input to output.
  • All the network's knowledge lives in its weights — the numbers on the connections.
  • It's not a real brain and not conscious — just a huge pile of multiply-add-and-decide steps working together.
