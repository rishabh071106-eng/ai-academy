Class Ten

How a Network Learns

Practice makes better — getting closer by measuring your mistake, then nudging your aim a tiny bit at a time.

Last class, the forest built its very first neural network — a web of little decision-makers all wired together. But a fresh network is like a brand-new student: full of connections, and absolutely terrible at everything. Its weights — the dials that say how loudly each connection matters — start out as random numbers. Today the class had one job: teach the network to actually be good.

For this lesson, Professor Hoot set up an archery range in the meadow. Tess the tortoise volunteered first. She is slow, careful, and never rushes — which, it turns out, is exactly the right personality for learning.

Tess drew the bow and let an arrow fly. It thudded into the grass, a good arm's length to the left of the target.

Bolt: “Ha! A miss! Let me try — I'll just fire a hundred arrows really fast and one of them is bound to hit.”

Professor Hoot: “Patience, Bolt. Watch what Tess does next. She is not going to fire blindly. She is going to study her mistake.”

Tess looked hard at where the arrow had landed. She asked herself two questions, and only two: How far off was I? and Which direction? Too far left, by a lot. So she nudged her aim a little to the right — not all the way, just a small, careful adjustment — and shot again.

This time the arrow landed left of the bullseye too, but closer. Less wrong. Tess nudged right again, by an even smaller amount. Closer still. Shot after patient shot, the misses shrank and shrank until — thunk — an arrow buried itself dead in the red center.

Mango: “She didn't get lucky… she got less wrong every single time!”

Professor Hoot: “Precisely. And that loop — try, measure how wrong you are, adjust a little, repeat — is exactly how a neural network learns. Tess just showed us the whole secret.”

Measuring the mistake

Here is the part most people miss: Tess couldn't improve until she could measure her mistake as a number. “A bit to the left” is a feeling. “Forty centimeters to the left” is something you can shrink. A network needs the same thing — a single number that says how wrong its guess was. The bigger the number, the worse the guess. The whole goal of learning is to make that number as small as it can possibly be.

🔍 How It Really Works

A neural network learns by repeating four steps, over and over:

1. Guess. You feed in an example, and the network — using its current weights — produces an answer. At first the answer is basically random.

2. Measure the mistake. We compare the guess to the correct answer and boil the difference down to one number called the loss (also called the error). High loss means a bad guess. We want loss as close to zero as possible.

3. Figure out which way is “downhill.” For each weight, we ask: if I nudged this dial up a hair, would the loss get bigger or smaller? That answer — the direction that makes the loss shrink — is what mathematicians call the gradient. Following it is like standing on a foggy hill and feeling with your foot for which way slopes downward, then stepping that way. That's why the method is named gradient descent: descend the error.

4. Nudge a little, then repeat. We tweak every weight a small step in its downhill direction. How big a step? That's the learning rate. Then we do it all again with the next example. One full pass through all the examples is called an epoch, and a network usually needs many epochs to get good.

The clever trick that figures out the downhill direction for every weight at once — even the ones buried deep in the middle of the network — is called backpropagation. It starts at the final error and works backward through the layers, handing each weight its share of the blame. Then gradient descent nudges them all. That's the engine. Everything else is repetition.

Notice the steps in the picture get smaller near the bottom — just like Tess's nudges got tinier as her arrows closed in on the bullseye. That's no accident. When the slope is steep, the network is very wrong, so it takes a bigger step. As the valley flattens out near the best answer, the steps naturally shrink, and the network settles gently into place instead of crashing past it.

Tess: “Slow and steady. I never tried to fix the whole mistake in one shot. I just made each shot a little less wrong than the last.”

✏️ Try It Yourself: Hotter / Colder

You already know this game — and it turns out you've been doing gradient descent your whole life. Grab a friend and a small hidden object (a coin, a toy).

  1. Your friend hides the object somewhere in the room while you cover your eyes.
  2. Now you walk around. Your friend's only job is to call out “warmer!” when you move closer and “colder!” when you move farther away. “Warmer/colder” is your loss — your measured error.
  3. Use the feedback: keep stepping in whatever direction makes it warmer. That direction is your gradient. The size of your steps is your learning rate.

Count how many moves it takes to find the object. Then play again taking giant leaps, and again taking tiny shuffles. Which gets there fastest? You're about to discover why the learning rate matters so much.

⚠️ Watch Out

The learning rate is a Goldilocks number — it has to be just right.

Set it too big and the network takes wild, giant leaps. It overshoots the bottom of the valley, flies up the far side, overshoots back, and bounces around forever without ever settling — like sprinting across the room and blowing right past the hidden coin every single time.

Set it too small and learning crawls. The network inches downhill so slowly it might take thousands of epochs to get anywhere — Tess's careful patience taken to a painful extreme.

And one more trap: a valley can have little dips that aren't the deepest spot. A network can roll into one of these and get stuck in a “pretty good but not best” place, thinking it's done. Researchers have clever tricks to nudge it back out.

📖 Word Bank
Loss (Error)
A single number that measures how wrong the network's guess was. Big loss means a bad guess; learning means shrinking it toward zero.
Gradient descent
The method of finding which way is “downhill” for the error and taking a small step that way — over and over — until the loss is as low as it can get.
Learning rate
How big each nudge is. Too big and it overshoots and bounces; too small and it learns painfully slowly.
Epoch
One full pass through all the training examples. A network usually needs many epochs to get good.
Backpropagation
The trick that works backward from the error to figure out how each weight, even the hidden ones, should be nudged.
💡 Big Idea

You don't fix a mistake all at once — you measure how wrong you are, step a little toward better, and repeat. Do that enough times and “terrible” quietly becomes “great.”

Before You Turn the Page…

  • A network learns by a patient loop: guess, measure the error, nudge the weights a little, repeat.
  • The loss turns “how wrong” into a single number we can shrink.
  • Gradient descent rolls the error downhill; backpropagation figures out how to nudge every weight.
  • The learning rate must be just right — too big overshoots, too small crawls.
