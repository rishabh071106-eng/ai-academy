You came in knowing nothing about AI. You're leaving able to make it — here's how to take the very first step.

What an AI maker is really made of

You might think you need a giant brain, a wall of glowing screens, and a mountain of math before you're allowed to make AI. You don't. The real ingredients are simpler, and you already have every one of them:

Curiosity. Every chapter in this book began with somebody asking “why?” or “how?” — usually Mango. Makers never stop. Pattern-spotting, Ziggy's gift: noticing that these things go together and those don't is the heart of nearly every algorithm you met. A friendship with a little math and code — not a fear of it. You can start with free, kid-friendly tools that do the heavy lifting while you learn. Patience to try, fail, and adjust, exactly like Tess the tortoise — and exactly like gradient descent, which improves by taking one small, careful step downhill at a time. And finally kindness, the lesson from your last two classes: a maker is responsible for what they build.

🚀 How To Really Start

Making your first model is not so different from everything you've already practiced in this book. It's a loop with four steps, and you can run it this week:

1. Collect examples. Want a model that tells cats from dogs? Gather lots of pictures of each — the more, the better. These examples are the lessons your model learns from.

2. Train. Show the examples to a free, browser-based tool — the kind where you teach a model by holding things up to a camera or dropping in pictures. It quietly adjusts itself to fit your examples, just like the networks in Class 7.

3. Test. Show it something brand-new it has never seen. Did it guess right? This is how you find out if it truly learned the pattern or just memorized — remember overfitting?

4. Improve. Wrong answers aren't failures; they're information. Add more examples, fix mislabeled ones, and train again. Collect → train → test → improve. Spin that loop, and you are officially an AI maker.

So where do you actually begin? Here are real next steps you can take, starting today. Try a free teachable-machine-style tool — the kind that lets you train a model right in a web browser just by showing it examples, no code required. Play with block-based coding, where you snap colorful blocks together to build programs (it's how thousands of makers wrote their very first one). Turn a question you're curious about into a science-fair project. Ask a teacher or a parent to explore alongside you. And most powerful of all, keep an ideas notebook: every time you think “I wish a computer could…”, write it down. That notebook is a list of things you might one day build.

Professor Hoot: “One more rule, and never forget it: anything you do on a computer or online, do it together with a grown-up you trust.”

✏️ Try It Yourself: Your First Model This Week

With a trusted adult, find a free browser tool that trains a model from a webcam, and teach it a tiny job:

  1. Pick two simple things to tell apart — thumbs-up versus thumbs-down, or hat on versus hat off.
  2. Collect lots of examples of each by holding them to the camera. More angles and more light make a stronger model.
  3. Train it, then test it with a brand-new pose it never saw. Keep notes in your ideas notebook.
  4. When it gets one wrong, don't groan — improve it. Add more examples and train again.

That's it. You just collected, trained, tested, and improved a real machine learning model. Welcome to the workshop.

⚠️ Watch Out: The Maker's Promise

Everything you learned in Classes 19 and 20 now belongs to you. The moment you can build AI, you also become responsible for it. Will your examples be fair, or will they leave whole groups out? Will your model be honest about what it can't do? Could it hurt or trick someone? A real maker asks these questions before hitting “train,” not after. Great power, real duty — be fair, be honest, be safe.

📖 Word Bank
Maker
Someone who doesn't just use technology but builds it. Starting today, that's you.
Model
The thing you train — the part of an AI that has learned a pattern from examples and can make guesses on something new.
Iterate
To go around the loop again, a little better each time. Try, fail, adjust, repeat — the maker's heartbeat.
Ethics
The set of questions about right and wrong that a responsible maker asks before, during, and after building.
