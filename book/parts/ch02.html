Class Two

How Machines Learn

Why we teach machines by showing them examples instead of telling them every single rule.

Professor Hoot tapped the chalkboard and beamed at the class. “Today,” he announced, “you are going to teach me something. Write down the rules — the exact rules — for recognizing a cat. When you're done, I'll follow them perfectly, like a machine. If your rules are good, I'll never be wrong.”

Bolt's paw shot up before Hoot had even finished. “Easy! Four legs, whiskers, and fur. Done.” He folded his arms, pleased with himself.

Professor Hoot: “Four legs, whiskers, fur. Wonderful.” He drew a wobbly dog on the board. “But Rex has four legs, whiskers, and fur. By your rule, Rex is a cat.”

The class giggled. Rex, who had been napping, lifted one ear at the sound of his name.

Pip the mouse, who almost never spoke, had been quietly studying the picture cards on her desk. She noticed something. “Professor… the rule breaks the other way too.” She held up a card of a cat curled into a sleeping ball. “This is a cat. But you can't see any of its four legs. They're tucked underneath.”

Professor Hoot: “Ah. Pip notices the tiny things, as always. So our rule says ‘cat’ for a dog, and ‘not a cat’ for a sleeping cat. Let's try to patch it.”

So they tried. Pointy ears! (Dogs have those.) Says meow! (You can't hear a photograph.) Small and soft! (So is a rabbit, and Bolt took mild offense.) Every single rule they wrote down was either too strict, too loose, or broke the moment someone found a sneaky exception. The board filled up with crossed-out rules until it looked like a tic-tac-toe disaster.

Pip: “There are too many kinds of cats. Fat ones, hairless ones, kittens, cats in funny hats. We'd need a million rules — and we'd still miss one.”

The better way: show, don't tell

Professor Hoot smiled the smile of an owl who had been waiting for exactly this moment. He swept the broken rules off the board and set down a tall stack of cards instead.

Professor Hoot: “Then let's stop telling. Pip, don't try to write the rule. Just look. I'll show you hundreds of pictures. Each one I'll tell you: this is a cat… this is not a cat… cat… not a cat… Your only job is to watch and let your brain find the pattern by itself.”

Pip looked. And looked. Card after card after card. At first she guessed randomly. But after a hundred examples, something clicked — not a rule she could say out loud, but a feeling for cat-ness she could now recognize instantly. Hoot held up a brand-new picture she had never seen. A cat in a raincoat. “Cat,” said Pip, without hesitation. She was right.

Pip: “I didn't memorize that picture — I'd never seen it! I just… learned what cats look like from all the others.”

Professor Hoot: “And that, class, is how machines learn. Not from rules we type in, but from examples we show them.”

🔍 How It Really Works

What Pip did has a real name: supervised learning. The word supervised means each example comes with the right answer attached — a label. A photo labeled “cat.” A photo labeled “not cat.” The machine always gets to peek at the correct answer while it studies.

Inside the machine there's a thing called a model — think of it as a big adjustable guessing-machine full of knobs. At the start, every knob is set randomly, so the model guesses like Pip on her first card: pure luck. Then training begins. The machine looks at an example, makes a guess, and checks the label. Wrong? It nudges its knobs a little so that next time it would be closer to right. Right? Good — keep going. Do this across thousands of labeled examples and the knobs slowly settle into a pattern that works.

Here's the part people miss: the real goal is not to ace the examples it already studied. The goal is generalization — getting new, never-seen examples right, like the cat in the raincoat. A model that only knows its practice cards has memorized, not learned.

So there are two ways to make a computer do a job. The old way is programming with rules: a human writes down every step (“if four legs and whiskers, then cat”). The new way is learning from data: you don't write the rule at all — you show examples and let the model discover the pattern itself. For messy things like cats, faces, or voices, learning from data wins by a mile.

Notice what the model never receives in that last step: a label. The new cat arrives bare, and the model must decide. That moment — guessing the answer for something fresh — is the whole reason we trained it in the first place.

✏️ Try It Yourself: Train Your Friend on “Blorps”

You're going to be Professor Hoot, and your friend is going to be the machine. The trick: you must never tell them the rule.

  1. Secretly invent a category called a blorp. Pick a rule only you know — say, “a doodle is a blorp if it has exactly three dots inside it.” Don't write the rule down where your friend can see.
  2. Draw about eight doodles. Label each one out loud: “This is a blorp… this is NOT a blorp…” Make some blorps and some non-blorps so the pattern is there to be found. These are your training examples.
  3. Now the test. Draw five new doodles your friend has never seen and ask: “Blorp or not?” Don't tell them the answers yet.
  4. Check their guesses. If they nailed the new ones, they generalized — they learned your rule from examples alone, exactly like a machine.

Play it again with a trickier rule (“a blorp has a curvy edge AND is colored in”) and watch how many more examples your friend needs to crack it.

⚠️ Watch Out

The machine learns whatever pattern is actually in your examples — even one you didn't mean to teach. If every cat you show is orange, the model may quietly decide “cat = orange,” and then call a pumpkin a cat and a black cat a dog. And if you show too few examples, it grabs the first flimsy pattern it finds and sticks with it. Garbage examples in, garbage learning out. We'll dig into this in Class Three (where good data comes from) and again in the class on overfitting, where a model memorizes instead of generalizing.

📖 Word Bank
Label
The correct answer attached to an example — “cat” or “not cat.” In supervised learning, every training example carries one.
Training
The practice phase: the model guesses, checks the label, and adjusts itself to be a little less wrong each time.
Model
The adjustable guessing-machine the computer builds. Training is what shapes it; afterward it's what makes the predictions.
Supervised learning
Learning from examples that already come with the right answers, so the machine can check its work as it goes.
Generalization
The real goal — getting new, unseen examples right, instead of just memorizing the ones you trained on.
💡 Big Idea

We don't teach a machine by writing down every rule — we show it labeled examples and let it find the rule itself.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • Some things are too messy for rules — every “cat rule” you write down breaks on some sneaky exception.
  • Instead, you show the machine many labeled examples and let it find the pattern — that's supervised learning.
  • Training means nudging the model over and over until its guesses match the labels.
  • The point isn't to memorize the examples — it's to generalize to new ones it's never seen.
