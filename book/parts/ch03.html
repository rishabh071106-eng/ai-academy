Class Three

Data: The Food That Feeds AI

An AI is only as good as what you feed it — so be very careful what goes in the basket.

Field trip day! Professor Hoot lined up the class at the edge of the forest, handed each student an empty basket, and announced the mission: “Today we are going to teach a machine to tell an oak leaf from a maple leaf. But a machine has never seen a leaf in its life. So first, we must bring it examples — lots and lots of leaves, each one labeled with its name. We call all those examples data.”

Mango: “Examples? That's easy! I can collect a HUNDRED leaves before lunch!” And off he scampered, swinging through the branches.

Mango was fast. But Mango was also a little careless. He grabbed only the big leaves, because big ones were easy to spot from up in the trees. He only collected on the sunny side of the path, because that's where the swinging was fun. And once or twice, in a hurry, he dropped a maple leaf into the “oak” pile without really checking.

Mango: “Done! A whole basket! Best collector in the class!”

Tess the tortoise had collected far fewer leaves — but she had done it differently. She took big leaves and small ones. She walked the shady side of the path as well as the sunny side. And for every single leaf, she stopped, looked carefully, and wrote the correct name on a little tag before dropping it in.

Professor Hoot: “Mango, you brought us a lot of leaves. Tess, you brought us careful ones. Let's feed both baskets to the machine and see which one teaches it better.”

Garbage in, garbage out

The machine studied Mango's basket first. Then Professor Hoot held up a small oak leaf from the shady part of the forest and asked the machine to name it. The machine thought hard and answered: “Maple!” Wrong. It tried another small leaf. “Maple!” Wrong again.

Mango: “What?! I gave it a hundred leaves! Why is it so bad?”

Professor Hoot: “Because every leaf you gave it was big and from the sunny side — and a few were labeled wrong. The machine didn't learn what an oak leaf is. It learned that ‘oak means big and bright.’ Show it a small leaf in the shade, and it's lost.” He ruffled his feathers. “There's an old saying for this, class. Garbage in, garbage out.”

Then they fed it Tess's careful basket. Big leaves, small leaves, sunny ones, shady ones — every one correctly labeled. This time the machine named the small shady oak leaf right away. Fewer leaves, but better leaves, taught it far more.

🔍 How It Really Works

To a machine, every example is just a list of features — the measurable facts about a thing. A leaf's features might be its size, the shape of its edges (smooth, pointy, or wavy), and its color. Alongside the features we write the right answer, called the label: “oak” or “maple.” A whole pile of labeled examples is a dataset.

For the machine to learn the real pattern, that dataset has to be three things at once. It needs to be big enough — a handful of leaves won't reveal the rule. It needs to be varied — big and small, sunny and shady — so the machine sees the full range of what a leaf can be. And the labels need to be correct, because the machine trusts every label completely; one mislabeled leaf teaches it something false.

Here's the quiet danger Mango fell into. If your data only ever shows one kind of example, the machine becomes blind to the rest. Train it only on big leaves and it decides small leaves aren't really leaves. When that blindness ends up being unfair to certain things — or certain people — we call it bias. It's such a big deal that it gets its own class later; for now, just remember: lopsided data makes a lopsided machine.

One more trick the pros use: they don't feed the machine all the data. They hide some away. The part they show the machine is the training set; the hidden part is the test set. Later they quiz the machine with the leaves it never saw — that's the only honest way to know if it truly learned, or just memorized.

✏️ Try It Yourself: Build Your Own Dataset

You don't need a computer to make a real dataset. You need a table and ten things.

  1. Pick two kinds of something you can collect ten of: coins and buttons, oak and maple leaves, spoons and forks — five of each, ten total.
  2. Draw a table. Make a row for each object, and three feature columns at the top, like size, color, and shape.
  3. Fill in the features for every object — honestly! Then add one last column called Label and write what each thing actually is.
  4. Now the test of variety: did you collect only big coins, or a mix? Only shiny ones? If a friend handed you a tiny dull coin, would your table still describe it?

That's it — you just built a labeled dataset by hand. Feed a table like this to a machine and it can start learning the difference. The wider your variety, the smarter it gets.

⚠️ Watch Out

A dataset that's too small or too one-sided teaches the wrong lesson — and you won't notice until it's too late. Mango's machine looked fine on big sunny leaves, then fell apart the moment it met a small one in the shade. If you only train on one kind of example, the machine quietly decides that kind is the only kind. Always ask: “What does my data not include?” The leaves you forgot to collect are exactly the ones the machine will get wrong.

📖 Word Bank
Data
The examples we feed a machine so it can learn. To a computer, data is rows of facts and answers.
Feature
One describable fact about an example — a leaf's size, color, or edge shape. Features are the clues the machine learns from.
Label
The correct answer attached to an example — “oak” or “maple.” The machine trusts every label, so they must be right.
Dataset
A whole collection of labeled examples, usually arranged as a table: one row per example, one column per feature, plus the label.
Bias
When lopsided data makes a machine unfair or blind to things it rarely saw. We'll meet this troublemaker again in its own class.
💡 Big Idea

A machine never learns what you want it to learn — it learns whatever your data shows it. So feed it carefully.

Before You Turn the Page…

  • Data is the food of AI: examples described by features, each with a correct label.
  • Good data is plentiful, varied, and correctly labeled — quantity and quality.
  • Garbage in, garbage out: one-sided data makes a blind, biased machine.
  • Hide a test set the machine never sees, so you can check it learned for real.
