When AI Learned to See and Hear

Multimodal models don't just read text — they process images, audio, and video in a single system.

You've spent the last few chapters thinking about AI as a text machine. You type words, it types words back. That was accurate — until about 2023. Now the most capable models accept images, audio, and video as naturally as they accept a sentence. The text box became a everything box.

This isn't a gimmick bolted on after the fact. Multimodal understanding is rapidly becoming the default architecture. If you only understand text-based AI, you're already a generation behind. Let's fix that.

What "Multimodal" Actually Means

A modality is just a type of input or output: text, images, audio, video, even 3D point clouds. A multimodal model handles more than one of these — not by switching between separate systems, but by reasoning across them together.

Here's the important distinction: a multimodal model doesn't "look at a picture and then describe it in words" the way you might narrate a photo to a friend on the phone. Instead, it converts the image into the same kind of internal representation it uses for language, then processes everything in a shared reasoning space.

Think of it this way: a text-only model lives in a world of words. A multimodal model lives in a world of meaning — and meaning can arrive as pixels, waveforms, or sentences.

When you upload a photo of a restaurant receipt and ask "what tip should I leave for 20%?", the model isn't running OCR in one box and math in another. It's encoding the image into tokens — yes, the same kind of tokens you know from LLMs — and reasoning over those tokens alongside your text prompt in a single forward pass.

How Images Get Turned into Tokens

You already know that LLMs break text into tokens — chunks of words or characters that the model processes as numbers. Images go through an analogous step, but the tool is different: a vision encoder.

The most common approach uses a Vision Transformer (ViT). It chops an image into a grid of small patches — typically 16×16 or 14×14 pixels each — and treats each patch like a token. These patch tokens get projected into the same dimensional space as word tokens, so the language model's transformer layers can attend to both image patches and words simultaneously.

Some architectures, like those behind GPT-4o and Gemini, train the vision encoder and the language model together from scratch. Others bolt a pre-trained vision encoder onto a pre-trained LLM and train a small "bridge" layer to align them. Both work. The joint training approach tends to produce tighter integration — the model can reason more fluidly across modalities — but it's vastly more expensive.

A single high-resolution image might produce hundreds or even thousands of visual tokens. This is why multimodal queries eat through your context window fast, and why some models quietly downsample your images before processing them.

And Then Came Audio

Speech follows a similar playbook. Raw audio gets converted into a spectrogram — a visual representation of frequencies over time — or processed by a specialized audio encoder like Whisper. The encoder outputs a sequence of audio tokens that represent not just words but tone, pacing, emphasis, and even background sounds.

This is where things get genuinely interesting. Early "voice AI" was just a pipeline: speech-to-text → LLM → text-to-speech. Three separate systems duct-taped together. You'd lose everything that wasn't words — sarcasm, hesitation, emotion, the sound of a crying baby in the background.

Natively multimodal models skip the transcription step. They hear the audio directly. When you talk to GPT-4o or Gemini in voice mode, the model is (at least in principle) processing your actual speech signal, not a text transcript of it. That's why these systems can respond to how you say something, not just what you say.

Google's I/O 2026 showcased over 100 new announcements, many leaning heavily on multimodal capabilities in Gemini — a sign that the industry considers this the primary axis of model improvement right now, not just a side feature.

Why This Is Harder Than It Looks

Combining modalities creates problems that don't exist in text-only models.

Alignment between modalities. The model needs to understand that the word "dog" and a photo of a golden retriever and a bark sound all refer to related concepts. Getting these representations to live in the same neighborhood of the model's internal space requires enormous amounts of paired training data — images with captions, audio with transcripts, videos with descriptions.

Hallucination gets sneakier. A text-only model might invent a fake citation. A multimodal model might "see" text in an image that isn't there, or confidently describe objects in a photo that don't exist. Because you're less likely to double-check a model's reading of an image than its textual claims, these errors can slip past you more easily.

Compute costs multiply. A 1000-word prompt is maybe 1,300 tokens. A single image might add 1,000 more. A minute of audio? Potentially several thousand. Video is audio plus images at 24–30 frames per second. The context window fills up fast, and every token costs compute. This is a real engineering constraint, not a theoretical one.

What You Can Actually Do With This

Forget the demos where someone asks a model to describe a sunset photo. Here's where multimodal models are proving genuinely useful:

  • Document understanding: Upload a PDF with charts, tables, and footnotes. The model reads the text and interprets the graphs. No preprocessing needed.
  • Accessibility: Real-time audio description of surroundings for visually impaired users — not from a script, but from live camera input.
  • Code from sketches: Photograph a whiteboard wireframe, get a working UI prototype.
  • Medical imaging triage: Models analyzing X-rays or pathology slides alongside patient notes (with appropriate human oversight, obviously).
  • Multilingual voice agents: Processing speech directly means the model can handle accents, code-switching, and languages with limited text training data better than a pipeline approach.

The pattern is clear: any task where a human would naturally use their eyes and ears together with language is a task where multimodal models outperform text-only ones.

We're still early. Current models see images well but interpret video unevenly. They hear speech clearly but struggle with overlapping speakers or heavy background noise. The trajectory, though, is unmistakable. The next generation of AI doesn't just read your messages. It watches, listens, and — increasingly — understands what it perceives.

Tomorrow, you'll want to think carefully about what that means for trust and verification. But that's the next chapter.
