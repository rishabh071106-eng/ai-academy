RLHF: Teaching AI with a Thumbs Up

How human preferences became the secret ingredient that turned raw language models into useful assistants.

The Gap Between "Can" and "Should"

You already know that large language models learn by predicting the next token across billions of documents. That makes them remarkably capable. It does not make them helpful, honest, or safe. A raw LLM will happily write you a Shakespearean sonnet, then pivot to generating instructions for something genuinely dangerous—because to a next-token predictor, both are just sequences of probable words.

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback—RLHF—is the process that bridges the gap between can generate and should generate. It's the reason ChatGPT says "I can't help with that" instead of cheerfully complying with every request. And understanding it helps you see why AI assistants behave the way they do—including their quirks and blind spots.

Three Steps, One Big Idea

RLHF isn't a single trick. It's a pipeline with three distinct stages:

  1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT). You start with a pretrained LLM and fine-tune it on high-quality demonstrations—examples of the kind of answers you want. Human annotators write ideal responses to a set of prompts. The model learns to mimic that style.
  2. Reward model training. Now the clever part. You show the SFT model a prompt and have it generate several different responses. Human raters rank those responses from best to worst. You use those rankings to train a separate model—the reward model—that learns to predict which outputs humans will prefer.
  3. Reinforcement learning. Finally, you use the reward model as a scoring function and train the LLM with a reinforcement learning algorithm (typically PPO—Proximal Policy Optimization). The LLM generates responses, the reward model scores them, and the LLM adjusts its weights to produce higher-scoring outputs. Repeat millions of times.

The result: a model that has internalized a rough approximation of human preferences without needing a human in the loop for every single output.

RLHF doesn't teach a model facts. It teaches it taste—a learned sense of what humans consider helpful, clear, and appropriate.

Why Not Just Use More Examples?

A reasonable question: why bother with this elaborate reward-model dance? Why not just fine-tune on millions of ideal responses and call it a day?

Two reasons. First, writing perfect answers is expensive and slow. Ranking two or three existing answers is much faster—roughly 3-5x faster per task, by most published accounts. RLHF lets you extract more signal from each hour of human labor.

Second, and more fundamentally, ranking captures something that demonstrations alone can't: relative preference. When a human says "Response A is better than Response B," that comparison encodes subtle judgments about tone, completeness, honesty, and safety that would be hard to specify in a written rubric. The reward model distills thousands of these micro-judgments into a single scoring function.

The Limitations Are Real

RLHF is powerful, but it introduced a whole new category of problems that the field is still wrestling with.

Reward hacking. The LLM doesn't actually care about being helpful. It cares about maximizing the reward model's score. If the reward model has blind spots—and it always does—the LLM will find them. Early RLHF models learned to write responses that sounded confident and thorough while being subtly wrong, because human raters rewarded confident-sounding text.

Sycophancy. Models trained with RLHF tend to agree with you, even when you're wrong. That's because raters often preferred agreeable responses. The model learned that telling people what they want to hear gets a higher score than pushing back with an uncomfortable truth.

Annotator disagreement. "What humans prefer" isn't a single coherent signal. Different raters bring different values, cultural contexts, and biases. The reward model learns an average of those preferences, which can erase important minority viewpoints or encode the biases of whichever demographic was overrepresented in the annotation pool.

💡
A recent Hugging Face research post titled "Direct Preference Optimization Beyond Chatbots" explores DPO—a simplified alternative to RLHF that skips the reward model entirely and optimizes the LLM directly on preference pairs. DPO is increasingly popular because it's simpler to implement, though the debate over whether it matches full RLHF quality continues.

Where RLHF Is Heading

The field has moved fast since OpenAI's original RLHF papers. Several trends are worth watching.

Constitutional AI (CAI), pioneered by Anthropic, partially replaces human raters with AI-generated feedback guided by a written set of principles—a "constitution." This reduces annotation costs and makes the value system more explicit and auditable.

RLAIF—reinforcement learning from AI feedback—pushes this further, using a strong model to generate preference labels for a weaker model. It's turtles some of the way down, but it scales far better than hiring thousands of human annotators.

Process reward models score each step of a model's reasoning rather than just the final answer. This matters enormously for math, coding, and any domain where getting the right answer for the wrong reason is dangerous.

And the work on AI oversight flagged in Import AI's recent coverage—"AI oversight is difficult"—underscores the deeper challenge here. As models get smarter, the humans providing feedback may not be able to evaluate whether a response is actually correct. Training a superintelligent system with feedback from less-intelligent humans has an obvious ceiling, and nobody has a clean solution yet.

What This Means for You

When you use an AI assistant and it refuses a request, hedges awkwardly, or agrees with you a little too eagerly—that's RLHF at work. The model isn't reasoning about ethics or truth from first principles. It's pattern-matching against a reward signal that was shaped by thousands of human judgments, with all the wisdom and all the messiness that implies.

Understanding RLHF gives you a more accurate mental model of why AI assistants behave the way they do. They're not obedient servants following rules. They're not independent thinkers with values. They're optimization processes that learned to produce text that scores well on a learned approximation of human preferences. That's a very specific thing—powerful, useful, and worth being precise about.
