How LLMs actually work

A plain-English walkthrough of what happens between you pressing Send and getting a response. Zero matrix equations.

You type "Explain gravity to a 5-year-old" into Claude and half a second later, a charming little explanation appears. What actually happened in that half-second?

Let's walk through it, piece by piece.

Step 1: Your words become numbers

Computers can't reason about letters. They reason about numbers. So the first thing an LLM does is tokenize your input — chop it into small pieces and replace each piece with an ID number.

A token is usually a word or a fragment of a word. "Explain" might be one token. "gravity" one token. Unusual words like "antidisestablishmentarianism" get split into many.

Rule of thumb: 1 token ≈ 0.75 English words. A 100-word paragraph is about 130 tokens.

Your prompt "Explain gravity to a 5-year-old" becomes something like: [43058, 17408, 311, 264, 220, 20, 4771, 6418]. A vector of integers. That's what the model sees.

Step 2: Numbers become meaning

Each token ID is then converted to a long list of numbers called an embedding — typically 1,000 to 12,000 numbers per token. These numbers aren't arbitrary. They encode meaning.

The model learned during training that "king" and "queen" have embeddings close together. "King" and "refrigerator" are far apart. This is how machines represent semantic relatedness.

🧠
The trick of embeddings: they turn meaning into geometry. Synonyms cluster. Opposites sit on opposite sides. Analogies become vector arithmetic. "King - Man + Woman" lands very close to "Queen." Weird, but true.

Step 3: Attention figures out what matters

Now the real magic. The model looks at every token in your prompt and, for each one, decides which other tokens are relevant to it.

In "The cat sat on the mat because it was tired" — what does "it" refer to? You know it's "the cat" because you understood the sentence. The model uses attention: for the token "it", it computes a weighted relevance score to every other token. "Cat" scores high. "Mat" scores lower. "Because" scores medium.

This happens across many layers (GPT-4-class models have 100+ layers), and each layer refines the representation. By the end, every token has been contextualized by every other token.

Step 4: Predict the next token

After all that processing, the model outputs a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary (typically 50,000 to 200,000 tokens).

For your prompt, the model might output something like:

"Imagine" → 12%
"Gravity"  →  8%
"Okay"     →  6%
"Think"    →  5%
...
"Banana"   →  0.0001%

The model picks one (usually the most likely, with some randomness controlled by a "temperature" parameter). Say it picks "Imagine." Now that token is appended to the input, and the whole process runs again to predict the next token. And again. And again. Until it decides to stop.

That's it. Really.

That's the whole game. Tokenize → embed → attend → predict → append → repeat. A state-of-the-art LLM is this loop, run with hundreds of billions of parameters that were tuned during training.

Where does the "intelligence" come from?

Short answer: the weights. Those hundreds of billions of numbers, adjusted over months of training on most of the readable internet, encode patterns dense enough that predicting the next token well requires reasoning-like behavior.

To predict the next word in "The murderer in the detective novel turned out to be the..." you have to kind of track the plot. To predict the next line of Python in a half-written function, you have to kind of understand what the function does. The training process forces the model to become good at these kinds of "kind of" cognitive tasks as a side effect of predicting text.

Intelligence, it turns out, may be what you get when you compress enough patterns tightly enough.

What this tells you about using LLMs

  • Context is the model's entire world. If something isn't in your prompt (or the chat history it can see), the model cannot use it. This is why prompt engineering matters.
  • Every token costs money and time. Longer prompts → slower, pricier. Be surgical.
  • The model has no memory between calls. Each API call is fresh. "Long conversations" work by resending the whole history every time.
  • Randomness is a knob. Low temperature = deterministic, repetitive. High temperature = creative, unpredictable. Pick for the task.
