Prompt engineering that works

Stop saying "please." Start giving structure, examples, and constraints. Here are the five patterns that reliably get better output.

Most people prompt LLMs the way they'd ask a coworker: vaguely, with a lot of assumed context. "Write me an email for this." "Summarize this." "Fix this code."

Then they're disappointed when the output is bland, generic, or wrong.

The fix isn't magic words. It's treating the prompt as a specification, not a wish.

Pattern 1: Role + Task + Format

The most reliable upgrade to any prompt is a three-part structure:

  1. Role: who is the model being, right now?
  2. Task: what exactly should it do?
  3. Format: what does the output look like?

Weak:

Summarize this article.

Strong:

You are a senior journalist writing for busy executives.

Summarize the article below in exactly 3 bullets.
Each bullet must:
- Start with a strong verb
- Be under 20 words
- End with the so-what for a business reader

Article:
{paste article}

Same model, same article, dramatically different output.

Pattern 2: Show, don't tell (few-shot examples)

Telling the model your style is weak. Showing it is strong. Paste 2-3 examples of exactly the output you want, and it will pattern-match.

Rewrite product descriptions in the brand voice.

Example 1:
Input: "Comfortable cotton t-shirt"
Output: "The kind of soft you forget you're wearing."

Example 2:
Input: "Durable leather wallet"
Output: "Built once. Carried forever."

Now rewrite:
Input: "Lightweight running shoes"
Output:

This single technique solves 70% of "the output doesn't sound right" problems.

Pattern 3: Chain of thought

Ask for reasoning before the answer. It's counterintuitive — shouldn't the model just give you the answer? — but getting the model to "think out loud" produces dramatically better results on anything involving logic, math, or multi-step decisions.

Question: A bat and ball cost $1.10 total.
The bat costs $1 more than the ball.
How much does the ball cost?

Think step by step, showing your work,
then give a final answer on the last line.

With no chain-of-thought, smaller models often say "10 cents" (wrong). With it, they catch the error.

📈
Why this works: remember from Chapter 2, the model predicts one token at a time. Each token it writes becomes context for the next one. If you force it to write out reasoning, it's literally giving itself more material to work with.

Pattern 4: Give it a place to fail gracefully

LLMs hallucinate when they don't know something. The fix: explicitly allow ignorance.

Weak:

Who is the current CEO of XYZ Corp?

Strong:

Who is the current CEO of XYZ Corp?

If you are not confident in your answer,
or if this information may have changed
after your training cutoff, say so explicitly
and do not guess.

Models are heavily trained to try to be helpful. That bias pushes them toward answering even when they shouldn't. An explicit permission slip to say "I don't know" counteracts it.

Pattern 5: Structured output

If you're going to feed the output into anything (another script, a database, a form), ask for structured output. JSON is a good default.

Extract details from the meeting transcript.

Respond in this exact JSON format:
{
  "attendees": [list of names],
  "decisions": [list of decisions made],
  "action_items": [
    {"owner": "name", "task": "what", "due": "when"}
  ],
  "open_questions": [list]
}

Transcript:
{paste}

Modern models will follow this structure with near-perfect reliability. Now you have something you can parse and use programmatically.

Five traps beginners fall into

  1. Politeness inflation. "Please could you kindly help me with..." adds tokens and no value. Be direct.
  2. Vague constraints. "Make it engaging" is unusable. "Use 2 sentences, active voice, one concrete statistic" is a spec.
  3. One mega-prompt for multi-step tasks. Break complex tasks into sequential prompts. Don't ask for a research report, an outline, and final writing in one shot.
  4. Treating the model like Google. Fact-lookup is what retrieval is for. Ask models to reason on material you provide, not recall trivia.
  5. Not iterating. Your first prompt is a draft. Test, see what's wrong, fix the instruction that caused the wrongness, retry. Prompting is debugging.

The meta-skill

The deep skill isn't memorizing patterns. It's developing a feel for what the model is likely to screw up and pre-empting it in the prompt.

Think of prompting like leaving instructions for a very intelligent but extremely literal intern who will never ask a clarifying question. Everything you don't specify, they'll invent. So specify.
