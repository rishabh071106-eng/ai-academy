1 · How does a bank actually make money?

Before loans, before pricing, before any fancy word — let's get the one idea everything else hangs on. If you understand this, the rest of banking suddenly makes sense.

A bank is a middleman for money. Some people have spare money and want it kept safe (and maybe earn a little). Other people need money now and are willing to pay for the privilege of borrowing it. The bank sits in the middle and connects the two.

In plain words

The bank pays savers a small rate (say 2–3%) and charges borrowers a bigger rate (say 4–5%). The difference — the “gap” — is roughly how the bank earns. Bankers call that gap the spread, or more precisely the net interest margin (NIM).

That's it. Deposits are the raw material (cheap money coming in). Loans are the product (money going out at a higher price). Everything else in this guide is detail hanging off that one idea.

Why this matters for your job

As a Product Owner building lending products, you're working on the “money going out” side — the part that earns the bigger rate. But you should always remember the loan is only profitable compared to what the money cost the bank to get in. Hold that thought; it comes back when we talk about pricing.

Two quick words you'll hear constantly

WordPlain meaning
DepositMoney customers put into the bank (savings, current account). To the bank this is actually a debt it owes you — it has to give it back.
Loan / FinancingMoney the bank gives out to a customer, to be paid back over time with extra on top. (“Financing” is just the word used for the Islamic version — same idea, we'll cover it.)

2 · Meet Aina (our story begins)

Our story

Aina is 29. She teaches science at a secondary school in Kuala Lumpur. For three years she's been renting a small flat, but she's tired of paying rent that buys her nothing. She's found an apartment she loves: RM 400,000. She has saved RM 50,000. She does not have RM 400,000. So she walks into Maybank — and our story begins.

We're going to follow Aina's loan from this first hopeful step all the way to the day she owns the apartment outright. Along the way, every stage of the loan lifecycle will appear naturally, and we'll stop to explain each idea gently and deeply.

In plain words: “loan lifecycle”

The loan lifecycle is just the life story of a loan — every stage it passes through, from “I'd like to borrow” to “fully paid off.” Like a person's life has childhood, school, work, retirement — a loan has application, approval, repayment, and ending. That's all the word means. Don't let it intimidate you.

Here is the whole journey on one line. We'll take it one stage at a time:

1. Apply → 2. Credit check → 3. Approve & price → 4. Sign & disburse → 5. Repay (servicing) → 6. Trouble (delinquency) → 7. Help (restructure) → 8. Recover → 9. Settle (done!)

Good news: most loans only ever touch stages 1–5 and then 9. Stages 6–8 are the “something went wrong” path. We'll learn them, but they're the exception, not the rule.

3 · Stage one — the dream & the application

Our story

Aina sits across from a Maybank officer (or, these days, opens the MAE app on her phone). She says: “I'd like to finance an apartment. It costs RM 400,000. I have RM 50,000 saved.” The officer smiles and starts asking questions — lots of them.

This first stage is origination — a fancy word that simply means “starting the loan.” The bank's goal here is to collect information. It needs to know two things: who is this person? and what exactly do they want?

First: who are you, really? (KYC)

Before lending a single ringgit, the bank must be sure Aina is really Aina — not a fraudster using a stolen identity. It checks her MyKad (the Malaysian national ID card). This identity check has a name:

In plain words: “KYC”

KYC = “Know Your Customer.” It's the bank confirming you are who you say you are, and that you're not a criminal laundering money. Think of it like airport security for banking — a little annoying, but it protects everyone. When it's done digitally (snap your IC, take a selfie), it's called e-KYC.

Second: can we check your money history? (consent + the credit check)

The officer asks Aina to give permission to look at her borrowing history. Why permission? Because the law (Malaysia's PDPA — Personal Data Protection Act) says a bank can't go digging into your personal data without your say-so.

Once Aina agrees, the bank pulls her credit report.

In plain words: “credit report & CCRIS”

A credit report is your borrowing report card. It shows every loan and credit card you've ever had and whether you paid on time. In Malaysia the main one is CCRIS, a database run by the central bank (Bank Negara Malaysia). Every bank reports to it and reads from it. There's also a private one called CTOS, which gives you a score — a single number summarising how trustworthy a borrower you are.

Our story

Aina's report comes back clean. Three years of credit-card payments, never late. A small car loan, paid perfectly. The bank likes what it sees. So far, so good.

Say this in the interview

“The application stage is really about capturing the right data and verifying identity — KYC, consent under PDPA, and pulling CCRIS and a CTOS score. As a Product Owner, the key decision here is which data points to capture, because whatever we don't capture now, we can't use to decide or report on later.”

4 · Stage two — “can she handle it?” (the credit check)

Now comes the heart of lending. The bank has Aina's information; it must decide whether to lend. This decision-making is called underwriting — an old word from insurance. Forget the word; remember the question it answers:

“Can she repay — and will she?”

Bankers have a famous checklist for this, taught for a hundred years: the Five Cs of Credit. Let's meet them through Aina.

The “C”The questionAina's case
CharacterDoes she pay people back? (willingness)Spotless credit report. ✔
CapacityCan her income handle the payments? (ability)Steady teacher's salary. ✔
CapitalDoes she have her own money in it?RM 50,000 saved. ✔
CollateralIf she can't pay, what can we sell?The apartment itself. ✔
ConditionsIs the economy / purpose sensible?A home to live in — sound. ✔

The most important number: can she afford it? (DSR)

Of the five Cs, Capacity is where most loans live or die. The bank works out: of the money Aina earns each month, how much is already promised to other debts — and is there enough left for this new home payment?

In plain words: “DSR”

DSR = Debt Service Ratio. It's the share of your monthly income already eaten by debt payments. If you earn RM 5,000 and pay RM 2,000 to loans, your DSR is 40%. Banks get nervous when this climbs too high (often above ~60–70%), because it means little breathing room. It's the single most important affordability check.

A quick number

Aina earns RM 6,000/month. Her only existing debt is a car loan of RM 700/month. The new home payment would be about RM 1,800/month (we'll see where that comes from later). So her total debt would be RM 2,500 out of RM 6,000 → a DSR of about 42%. Comfortable. The bank is happy.

Who actually decides — a person or a computer?

For a clean case like Aina's, a computer can decide in seconds using rules and a scoring model. For messier cases (a business owner with irregular income), a human underwriter looks closely. Most banks do both: auto-approve the easy “yes,” auto-reject the obvious “no,” and send the tricky middle to a person.

In plain words: “rating & scorecard”

A scorecard is the model that turns Aina's data into a number — like a credit score, but the bank's own. From that, the bank gives her a risk rating (a grade, like a school grade A to F). A good grade means “low chance of not paying.” Remember this grade — in Part 3 it will directly decide the price of her loan. This is the secret link between the credit check and the price tag.

Say this in the interview

“Underwriting answers can and will they repay, using the Five Cs — and in practice the make-or-break number is the DSR. The output is a risk rating, and that rating is what later drives the pricing. So credit assessment and pricing aren't two separate things — they're one chain.”

5 · Stage three — the yes, and the price tag

Our story

The decision comes back: approved. But there are two details. First, the bank won't lend the full RM 400,000 — it asks Aina to put down 10% herself, so it will lend RM 360,000. Second, it tells her the interest rate: 4.40% per year. The bank prints all this in a formal letter called the Letter of Offer.

Two new ideas just appeared. Let's unpack both.

Why only RM 360,000? (the down payment & “LTV”)

The apartment costs RM 400,000. The bank lends RM 360,000. Aina pays the other RM 40,000 herself (plus she keeps RM 10,000 of her savings for fees). That RM 40,000 is the down payment — her own “skin in the game.”

In plain words: “LTV / Margin of Finance”

LTV = Loan-to-Value. It's the loan as a percentage of the thing's value. RM 360,000 loan ÷ RM 400,000 apartment = 90% LTV. (In Malaysia this is often called Margin of Finance.) Why does the bank care? Because if Aina ever stops paying and the bank must sell the apartment, that 10% cushion protects it from loss. Lower LTV = safer for the bank.

The 4.40% — where does a price even come from?

This is the question you specifically wanted answered, and it deserves its own deep section. For now, just hold this teaser:

The big reveal (coming in Part 3)

Aina's 4.40% is not random and not the same for everyone. It is built up from what the money costs the bank, plus a little for running costs, plus a little extra to cover the risk that some borrowers won't repay, plus the bank's profit. Because Aina is low risk, her rate is low. A riskier borrower would be quoted a higher rate. We'll build her exact number, piece by piece, in Part 3.

6 · Stage four — signing & getting the money

Our story

Aina accepts the offer. She signs a stack of documents, the most important being a promise — in writing — to repay. The bank also registers a legal claim over the apartment. Then it pays the money to the seller. The apartment is hers to live in — though not yet fully hers to own.

Two important things happened: a promise and a claim.

In plain words: the two key documents

1. The promise to repay. Aina signs a legal document saying she'll pay the money back on the agreed terms. If she breaks it, the bank can take her to court.

2. The claim on the apartment (the “charge” / lien). The bank registers a legal hold over the apartment. This means: Aina lives there and it's “hers,” but she can't sell it without settling the bank first, and if she stops paying, the bank has the right to take and sell it. In Malaysia this is a charge under the National Land Code. The general word is a lien.

In plain words: “collateral”

Collateral is simply the thing the bank can take if you don't pay. Here, it's the apartment. A loan with collateral (home, car) is a secured loan — safer for the bank, so cheaper for you. A loan with no collateral (a personal loan, a credit card) is unsecured — riskier for the bank, so more expensive. This is your first hint about why different loans cost different amounts.

In plain words: “disbursement”

Disbursement just means “the bank actually pays out the money.” Notice it usually goes to the seller, not into Aina's hand. The day this happens, the loan officially “comes alive” and starts counting. (For a house still being built, the bank pays in stages as construction progresses — called progressive disbursement.)

Say this in the interview

“At disbursement the loan becomes a real, live asset on the bank's books. The product/operational care here is getting the documentation, the lien perfection, and the money movement right — and then ‘boarding' the loan onto the servicing system so repayments can begin. A mistake here is expensive and hard to undo.”

7 · Stage five — the long, quiet middle (paying it back)

Our story

Every month for the next 30 years, RM 1,803 leaves Aina's account automatically and goes to the bank. Month after month, year after year. This quiet, faithful repayment is where a loan spends almost its entire life.

This stage is called servicing — “looking after the loan” while it's being repaid. It's unglamorous but it's where most banking software actually lives, so as a future Product Owner, pay attention here.

In plain words: “installment”

An installment is the fixed amount Aina pays each month (RM 1,803). Here's the surprising part that confuses everyone at first: that single payment is secretly split into two parts — some of it pays the bank's interest (the rent on the money), and the rest reduces what she actually owes (the principal).

The magic of the shrinking interest (amortization)

Here's something beautiful and worth truly understanding. In the early years, most of Aina's RM 1,803 is interest — because she still owes a lot, so the “rent” is high. But every month she chips away at what she owes, so next month's interest is a tiny bit smaller, and a tiny bit more of her payment goes to actually shrinking the debt. Over time this snowballs.

In plain words: “amortization”

Amortization is just the name for this gradual paying-off — the same monthly payment, but slowly shifting from “mostly interest” to “mostly paying down the debt,” until one day it hits zero. You don't need the formula; you need the picture above. That intuition alone will carry you in an interview.

What else happens during servicing?

  • Collecting the payment — usually an automatic monthly deduction (a “standing instruction”).
  • Splitting it correctly — interest vs principal, every single month.
  • Sending statements — so Aina sees her balance shrinking.
  • Reporting to CCRIS — every month the bank tells the central database “Aina paid on time,” which keeps her credit report healthy.
Say this in the interview

“Servicing is the longest stage and where most platform software lives — payment processing, interest accrual, statements, and the payment ‘waterfall' that splits each installment into interest and principal. It's unglamorous, but it's the system of record for the customer's money, so correctness matters more than speed.”

8 · Stage six — when life goes wrong

Our story (an alternative path)

Imagine, five years in, Aina loses her job. One month, for the first time, the RM 1,803 doesn't arrive. Then a second month. The loan has entered a stage no one wants: delinquency.

In plain words: “delinquency & default”

Delinquent simply means “behind on payments.” Banks count it in days past due (DPD) or months in arrears. Miss one month = 30 days past due. Miss three months (~90 days) and the loan is usually called impaired or in default — a serious flag meaning the borrower has broken the agreement. (“Default” = the formal “you've broken the contract” line; “delinquent” = the softer “you're running late.”)

Why the bank acts fast

A missed payment is an early warning. The longer someone is behind, the less likely they are to ever catch up. So banks watch a number called the roll rate — the chance a loan “rolls” from 1 month late to 2, to 3. Catching trouble early (a friendly reminder, a phone call) is far cheaper than dealing with it late. This early, gentle chasing is called collections.

Watch out (a fairness rule)

Chasing payments is tightly regulated. The bank can't harass, threaten, or call at all hours. Treating a struggling customer with dignity isn't just nice — it's the law, and it's the “Humanising Financial Services” mission you'll hear Maybank repeat. Remember that link for the interview.

9 · Stage seven — a helping hand

Here's something that surprises beginners: when a borrower is in trouble, the bank usually doesn't want to grab the house. Taking and selling collateral is slow, expensive, and recovers less than the loan. The bank often makes more money by helping the borrower keep paying, even at a reduced amount. So it offers a lifeline.

In plain words: the rescue tools

This stage is called loss mitigation (“reducing the loss”) or, in Malaysia, R&R — Rescheduling & Restructuring. The tools:

  • Payment holiday / moratorium — pause payments for a few months while the borrower gets back on their feet.
  • Reschedule — stretch the loan over more years so each monthly payment becomes smaller.
  • Restructure — change the terms more deeply (e.g. a lower rate for a while).

Our story

Aina calls the bank and explains. They stretch her remaining loan over a few more years, dropping her payment to something she can manage with her new, smaller salary. She's relieved. The bank keeps a paying customer. Both win. (Malaysia even has a free national agency, AKPK, that helps people restructure their debts.)

Say this in the interview

“Loss mitigation is an economic decision, not a charity. Foreclosing recovers less than the loan and costs a lot, so a restructure that keeps the customer paying often gives the bank a better outcome — and it fits the ‘Humanising' mission. The product challenge is spotting hardship early and offering the right option quickly.”

10 · Stage eight & nine — the hard road, and the happy ending

The hard road (recovery) — only if all else fails

If a borrower truly can't pay even after help, the bank reaches the last resort: it uses the collateral. For a home, this is foreclosure — a legal process (in Malaysia, a court “order for sale”) ending in the apartment being sold at auction. For a car, it's repossession. Whatever the sale brings in reduces the debt.

In plain words: “charge-off & provisions”

If a loan looks unlikely to be fully recovered, the bank writes it down in its accounts — admitting the loss. This is a charge-off. Importantly, the customer can still legally owe the money; it's an accounting step, not forgiveness. Banks also set aside money in advance for expected losses, called provisions (under a rule called ECL / MFRS 9 — “expected credit loss”). Think of it as the bank keeping a rainy-day fund for loans that might go bad.

The happy ending (settlement) — the normal path

Our story (back to the good timeline)

Aina never lost her job. For 30 years she paid faithfully. One morning she makes her final payment. The bank releases its claim on the apartment — files a discharge of charge — and for the first time, the apartment is 100% hers, owned free and clear. The loan's life is complete.

In plain words: “settlement & payoff”

Settlement (or payoff) is simply the loan being fully paid and closed. It can happen three ways: paying the last scheduled installment (Aina), paying it all off early, or refinancing (taking a new loan elsewhere to pay off this one). However it ends, the bank removes its lien and reports the loan as “settled” — a gold star on the customer's credit report.

Zoom out — you now know the whole journey

Apply → credit check → approve & price → sign & disburse → repay → (trouble → help → recover) → settle. That's the entire loan lifecycle, and you understood every word. Take a breath — the hard part is done. Now let's answer your big question: how is the price decided?

11 · Why doesn't everyone pay the same rate?

You asked specifically about pricing, so we'll go slowly and build it from the ground up. Start with a question that unlocks everything:

Why does a risky borrower pay a higher interest rate?

Here's the intuition, using something you already understand: car insurance.

The insurance analogy

A 19-year-old with two accidents pays more for car insurance than a careful 45-year-old. Why? Because the insurer expects to pay out more often for the risky driver, so it charges more upfront to cover that. Lending works exactly the same way. A borrower more likely to stop paying will, on average, cost the bank more — so the bank charges them a higher rate to cover that expected cost. That's the entire idea behind risk-based pricing: price the loan to match the risk.

The fairness inside it

It can feel unfair that struggling borrowers pay more. But think of it as a pool: the bank lends to many people; a few won't repay. The extra charged for risk is what covers those few losses — paid by the pool, so the bank stays solvent and can keep lending to everyone. Without risk-based pricing, safe borrowers would be overcharged to subsidise risky ones, or the bank would simply refuse risky borrowers entirely.

12 · What “interest” really is

Before we build a price, let's be crystal clear on the thing being priced.

In plain words: “interest”

Interest is rent on money. When you rent an apartment, you pay the owner for using their space for a while. When you borrow money, you pay the bank rent for using their money for a while. The interest rate (say 4.40% per year) is the rent expressed as a yearly percentage of what you've borrowed. Borrow RM 100,000 at 4.40% and the first year's rent is about RM 4,400.

One more: “rate” vs “APR”

You'll see two numbers. The interest rate is the rent on the money. The APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is the rent plus the fees, rolled into one yearly percentage — so it shows the true all-in cost and lets you compare loans fairly. APR is always a little higher than the plain rate. That's all.

Islamic version (important at Maybank)

Islamic finance forbids charging interest (called riba). So instead of lending money at interest, the bank does a real transaction — for a home, it co-buys the property with you and you gradually buy out its share, paying profit instead of interest. Same economics, different structure and words: “financing” not “loan,” “profit rate” not “interest rate.” Maybank is a global leader in this, so just knowing this paragraph will impress.

13 · The five ingredients of a loan's price

Now the main event. A loan's interest rate isn't plucked from the air — it's a recipe with five ingredients stacked on top of each other. We'll add them one by one to build Aina's 4.40%.

IngredientIn plain wordsAina
1. Cost of fundsWhat the bank paid to get the money (mostly the interest it pays savers). The floor under every loan.2.50%
2. Operating costThe cost of running the bank — staff, branches, the app, processing her loan.0.50%
3. Expected lossA small slice to cover the risk she (or borrowers like her) won't repay. This is the risk-based part.0.30%
4. Cost of capitalBanks must hold their own safety buffer against each loan; investors expect a return on it.0.70%
5. Profit marginThe bank's reward for doing all this.0.40%
Total interest rate4.40%
The key insight

Four of the five ingredients are roughly the same for everyone. Only one — the expected loss — changes a lot from person to person, depending on how risky they are. That single ingredient is where “risk-based pricing” actually happens. So to understand pricing differences, we just need to understand that one number. Let's do exactly that.

14 · Risk, in numbers (PD, LGD, EAD)

How does a bank turn “this person is a bit risky” into an actual percentage for ingredient #3? With three simple ideas. Don't be scared of the abbreviations — each is just one plain question.

TermThe plain question it answersAina (low risk)
PD
Probability of Default		“What's the chance they stop paying?”1% (1 in 100)
LGD
Loss Given Default		“If they stop, how much do we actually lose after selling the collateral?”25%
EAD
Exposure at Default		“How much do they owe us at that moment?”RM 360,000

Multiply the three together and you get the Expected Loss — the average amount the bank expects to lose on this loan, per year:

Worked: Aina's expected loss

Expected Loss = PD × LGD × EAD
= 1% × 25% × RM 360,000
= 0.01 × 0.25 × 360,000
= RM 900 per year

As a percentage of her RM 360,000 loan: 900 ÷ 360,000 = 0.25% ≈ the 0.30% we put in her price stack. That's where ingredient #3 came from.

Why collateral makes loans cheaper

Notice the LGD (loss given default) is only 25%, not 100%. Why? Because even if Aina stops paying, the bank can sell the apartment and recover most of the money. The collateral shrinks the loss. That's the deep reason a home loan is cheaper than a personal loan: a personal loan has no collateral, so its LGD is near 100% — the bank loses almost everything if it goes bad, so it charges much more to cover that bigger risk.

Say this in the interview

“The risk premium in a loan's price is the Expected Loss — Probability of Default times Loss Given Default times Exposure. So the chain is: the credit assessment produces a risk rating → the rating gives a PD → combined with collateral-driven LGD → that's the Expected Loss → which becomes the spread on top of the bank's base rate → the customer's price. Rating and pricing are one connected story.”

15 · Full worked example: Aina vs Ben

Now let's see risk-based pricing in action by introducing a second character.

Our story

Meet Ben. He wants the same apartment loan as Aina — RM 360,000 over 30 years. But Ben is self-employed; his income swings month to month, and his credit report shows two late payments last year. He's not a bad person — he's just a riskier borrower. Watch what happens to his price.

Everything is the same except the risk. Aina's chance of default was 1%; Ben's is higher — say 5%. The collateral is the same apartment, so LGD stays 25%. Let's recompute just the expected-loss ingredient:

Worked: Ben's expected loss

Ben = PD × LGD × EAD = 5% × 25% × 360,000 = RM 4,500 / year
As % of loan = 4,500 ÷ 360,000 = 1.25%

So Ben's risk ingredient is ~1.25%, versus Aina's ~0.25% — a full 1% more, purely because he's five times more likely to default.

Now stack both prices side by side. Only ingredient #3 changed:

IngredientAinaBen
Cost of funds2.50%2.50%
Operating cost0.50%0.50%
Expected loss (risk)0.30%1.30%
Cost of capital0.70%0.80%
Profit margin0.40%0.40%
Final rate4.40%5.50%

(Ben's cost of capital nudges up too, because banks must hold a bigger buffer against riskier loans — but the big mover is the risk premium.)

What that 1.1% does to real life

A one-percent difference sounds tiny. It isn't. Same loan, same apartment — here's the monthly payment each is quoted:

Aina @ 4.40%Ben @ 5.50%
Monthly payment≈ RM 1,803≈ RM 2,044
Extra per month—+ RM 241
Extra over 30 years—≈ + RM 87,000
The lesson, in one line

Ben pays about RM 87,000 more over the life of the loan than Aina — for the identical apartment — entirely because he was a riskier borrower. That is risk-based pricing. And it's why a clean credit history is worth real money: it literally lowers the price of everything you ever borrow.

Say this in the interview

“Risk-based pricing means two customers can get very different rates on the same product. The driver is the expected-loss component — set by the borrower's risk rating and the collateral. As a Product Owner I'd care that the pricing is explainable and fair: we must be able to tell a customer why they got their rate, and make sure the model isn't unfairly penalising protected groups.”

16 · How pricing links to the whole loan

Let's tie Part 3 back to the story, because the interview gold is showing you see the connections, not just the parts.

  • Pricing happens at the approval stage (Stage 3) — but it uses the data from the credit check (Stage 2). No credit assessment, no price.
  • The price sets the payment the customer lives with for years (Stage 5). A higher rate = a bigger monthly installment.
  • The price drives the bank's profit. Price too low and the bank loses money on risky loans; price too high and customers go to a competitor. Getting it right is a real product and business decision.
  • In Malaysia, the price is quoted as “SBR + spread.” The SBR (Standardised Base Rate) is a public base number set in line with the central bank's policy rate; the spread is the bit on top that carries the four other ingredients including the risk premium. When the central bank raises rates, the SBR rises, and floating-rate customers' payments go up.
In plain words: “fixed vs floating”

A fixed rate stays the same for the whole loan — predictable. A floating (variable) rate moves up and down with the SBR — so the payment can change over time. Most Malaysian home loans are floating.

17 · Core banking, in one simple picture

You'll hear “core banking” constantly. It sounds technical; it's actually simple.

In plain words: “core banking”

Core banking is the bank's master record-book. It's the central system that knows, for every customer: who they are, how much is in each account, every loan they have, and every transaction. When Aina's RM 1,803 leaves her account, the core is what records “payment received, balance updated.” It's the single source of truth for everyone's money — so it has to be rock-solid and is changed very carefully.

Everything customers touch — the MAE app, the website, the ATM — sits on top of the core and talks to it. The apps are the friendly face; the core is the trusted ledger underneath.

Say this in the interview

“The core is the system of record — we keep it stable and change it rarely, because errors in the ledger are catastrophic. Innovation happens in the digital layer on top, which talks to the core through APIs. That's exactly the kind of modernisation MBB Labs exists to do.”

18 · Where a Product Owner fits in

So where does your job sit in all of this? Right in the middle — as the person who decides what to build and why.

In plain words: “Product Owner”

A Product Owner (PO) owns a product (say, the digital home-financing journey in the MAE app). You decide what features get built, in what order, and why — based on customer needs and business value. You don't write the code; the engineers do. You're the bridge between the business (what we want to achieve), the customer (what they need), and the tech team (who build it). You also keep the risk and compliance people happy, because this is a bank.

The reassuring truth

A great Product Owner is not the person who knows the most banking jargon. It's the person who deeply understands the customer's problem, can prioritise ruthlessly, and can get a team aligned to ship something valuable. The banking knowledge in this guide is your foundation — but your product thinking and communication are what they're really hiring.

22 · The face-to-face with the Head of Product

Your next round is in person with the Head of Product Management — the person you'd ultimately report up to. This changes how you should prepare. Let's get you ready.

What this person actually cares about

A Head of Product is not trying to catch you out on definitions. They're senior; they care about how you think. Specifically: Do you obsess over the customer? Can you prioritise and make trade-offs? Can you handle ambiguity and stakeholders? Do you think in outcomes (did it help the customer / business?) rather than just output (did we ship it)? And — will you be good to work with and grow? Domain knowledge matters, but it's the floor, not the ceiling.

They're checking…How you show it

How to answer a behavioural question — the STAR method

In plain words: “STAR”

When asked “tell me about a time you…”, structure your story as STAR: Situation (set the scene briefly) → Task (what you needed to do) → Action (what you specifically did) → Result (the outcome, ideally a number). Keep it ~2 minutes. End on the result.

How to handle “I'm newer to banking”

You might be asked something you don't fully know. Don't bluff — senior people see through it instantly, and it breaks Integrity (a T.I.G.E.R. value). Instead: say what you do understand, reason from first principles, and show you learn fast. For example: “I'm still deepening my banking domain knowledge, but here's how I'd reason about it… and I've found I pick up domains quickly — for instance, to prepare for this role I worked through the full loan lifecycle and how risk-based pricing is built up.” That turns a gap into evidence of curiosity (another T.I.G.E.R. value) and effort.
